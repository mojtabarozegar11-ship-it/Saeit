# Historical-Release Sensitivity of Agricultural Value Added per Worker in World Development Indicators: A Three-Vintage Fixed-Country Audit

**Manuscript status:** revised research draft, 26 October 2026. Proposed human author: Mojtaba Roozegar (subject to real scientific contribution, verification and explicit final approval). This text is **not** an accepted or submitted journal article. No fabricated data, causal effects, significance tests or novelty priority are asserted.

## Abstract

**Background:** Historical revisions to World Development Indicators (WDI) can alter published agricultural productivity growth estimates. **Objective:** Quantify release sensitivity using fixed countries and distinguish revisions from changing observation horizons. **Methods:** We compared official October 2024, October 2025 and 2026 WDI sources for agricultural value added per worker, verified source hashes, and computed annualised 2000–2019 log growth for the same countries. Separate current-source ILOSTAT agricultural employment and WDI implied-worker comparisons assessed denominator consistency, not historical revision causality. **Results:** The 2026 snapshot covered 217 nonaggregate economies, including 153 with positive 2000/2019/2023 observations. The three-release 2000–2019 cohort contained 155 economies. Median annualised growth was 2.656%, 2.697% and 2.362% across the respective releases. Growth signs switched in 6, 6 and 10 economies across successive and end-to-end comparisons; only three end-to-end switches exceeded 0.5 log-percentage points per year in magnitude on both sides. End-to-end Spearman rank correlation was 0.887; 26/155 estimates changed by over one log-percentage point annually. The current-source ILOSTAT/WDI worker-level check matched 200 country-year records, with median absolute relative discrepancy 5.81%; only 73 of 157 WDI-positive two-endpoint economies entered the worker-growth comparison. **Conclusions:** Release revisions materially affect descriptive classifications, but selected-country agreement does not establish independent measurement, revision mechanisms, causality or methodological novelty.

**Keywords:** agricultural labor productivity; World Development Indicators; missing data; country panel; structural transformation; reproducibility.

## 1. Introduction

Agricultural labor productivity is a widely used aggregate indicator in development economics, but it is neither an unmediated observation of farm performance nor a comprehensive measure of rural welfare. In international statistics, sector value added must be defined and measured through national accounts, and agricultural employment is estimated from labor-market data that can vary in scope, timing and model-based adjustment. Cross-country differences in the indicator therefore combine economically meaningful variation with heterogeneous measurement systems. Even when all series are reported in constant US dollars, price-base comparability alone does not ensure that workers, hours, unpaid family labor and agricultural activities are observed consistently across countries.

The substantive economics of agricultural productivity is not new. Gutierrez (2002) examined productivity differences and conditional convergence across 44 countries. Eberhardt and Teal (2013) demonstrated that heterogeneous sectoral technologies complicate cross-country growth inference. Dieppe and Matsuoka (2021; subsequently published in 2025) analyzed sector-specific convergence and structural reallocation, including agricultural productivity. Gollin, Lagakos and Waugh (2013/2014) identified important discrepancies between aggregate sector value-added-per-worker measures and microeconomic measures of hours and human capital. This literature rules out presenting a simple new WDI trend chart as the first evidence of productivity growth or convergence.

A more limited and auditable problem is the relationship between **which countries have observations** and the summary statistic used to describe a global country panel. A period-specific complete-pair median may change merely because different economies enter each interval, even if the underlying individual-country trajectories are not compared consistently. Conversely, restricting to a balanced panel fixes the identity of countries but may exacerbate selection bias because data-poor economies are excluded. The present analysis does not purport to solve these problems. It records the data availability and tests the descriptive sensitivity of two intervals to a fixed-country restriction, using a dated, reproducible official-source extract.

The research questions are therefore deliberately narrow: (i) how many nonaggregate economies have valid agricultural value-added-per-worker observations in the selected WDI years; (ii) how do descriptive endpoint-growth statistics change under a fixed-country restriction; (iii) for an identical 155-economy 2000–2019 cohort, how much do archived October 2024, October 2025 and October 2026 WDI releases alter country-specific growth and its sign; and (iv) are any classification changes non-monotone across consecutive releases? These questions were refined during exploratory analysis and **were not preregistered**. No hypothesis of causal policy effects, farmer-level change or cross-country beta-convergence is tested.

## 2. Data and methods

### 2.1 Indicator and provenance

We used World Bank WDI indicator **NV.AGR.EMPL.KD**, described as *agriculture, forestry, and fishing, value added per worker (constant 2015 US$)*. The public WDI record identifies World Bank national accounts and ILOSTAT as sources. Values were retrieved from the official API for 2000, 2019 and 2023, along with the World Bank country metadata endpoint. The retrieved WDI indicator responses were labeled **last updated 8 October 2026**. The version-controlled observed-country panel is supplied as `AE-2026-001_OFFICIAL_WDI_OBSERVED_COUNTRY_PANEL_2026-10-25.csv`; this document reports a dated snapshot, not an immutable official release.

**Official indicator construction and construct validity.** The [World Bank WDI metadata glossary](https://databank.worldbank.org/metadataglossary/world-development-indicators/series/NV.AGR.EMPL.KD), checked on 25 October 2026, defines the numerator as sectoral value added (gross output less intermediate inputs) in constant **2015** US dollars, and the denominator as the **number employed in the sector**, drawn from the World Bank/ILOSTAT source chain. Its ISIC Rev. 4 coverage includes **agriculture, forestry and fishing (divisions 01–03)**, not crop agriculture alone. The companion [World Bank SDG-series methodological glossary](https://databank.worldbank.org/metadataglossary/sustainable-development-goals-%28sdgs%29/series/NV.AGR.EMPL.KD) explicitly identifies **ILO modelled employment estimates** and notes that employment counts include full-time and part-time workers. Thus this is not independently observed farm output per hour, total factor productivity, crop yield, or a survey of agricultural workers. Source-vintage changes in either the national-accounts numerator or ILO employment denominator may change country-level rates even without a comparable change in farm technology. The country sample and descriptive arithmetic below do **not** separately validate the ILO model or reconstruct the numerator from source national accounts; that limitation is a central measurement finding, not an assumption of independent corroboration.

We excluded entries with World Bank country-metadata `region.id = NA`, which represent aggregates, and retained 217 country/economy entries. This operational classification should not be confused with sovereign-state membership or with a fixed list of UN countries. Countries with missing indicator values remained in the denominator of the coverage table but were excluded from each paired-growth calculation. No missing value was set to zero or imputed. We accepted positive finite observations only because log changes are undefined for nonpositive endpoints.

### 2.2 Descriptive estimands

For economy `i` and endpoint `t` (2019 or 2023), let `y_it` denote reported constant-2015-dollar value added per agricultural worker. The annualized log change is

`g_i(2000,t) = [ln(y_it) - ln(y_i,2000)] / (t - 2000)`.

We report the arithmetic mean and median of `g_i` across eligible economies. The statistics are **equally weighted by economy**, not by agricultural workforce, production, population, irrigated area or economic output. They must not be described as the growth of global agriculture. The 2000–2019 endpoint comparison requires both 2000 and 2019 to be observed, while 2000–2023 requires 2000 and 2023. The balanced-sample sensitivity analysis requires all three years to be present and positive for the same economy.

The design is purely observational and descriptive. No population-sampling standard errors, formal global-representativeness significance tests, causal counterfactuals or time-series break adjustments were prespecified. Later, explicitly post hoc, country-level resampling and a conditional permutation diagnostic were computed for source-coverage sensitivity; these are not design-based population confidence intervals or causal tests. Regional summaries, where mentioned, are exploratory and use the official region field from the dated country metadata response.

### 2.4 Historical-release reconstruction and paired estimands

The three-vintage extension is a **post hoc, descriptive audit**, not a preregistered confirmatory analysis. The historical sources are the World Bank WDI Excel archives dated **24 October 2024** and **8 October 2025**; the comparison source is the separately archived **2026 official API snapshot**. The repository script `analysis/wdi_three_vintage_fixed_cohort_revisions.py` downloads the original archived ZIPs, checks source SHA256, reads the official Excel data, aligns indicator `NV.AGR.EMPL.KD` by economy code and endpoint year, and joins the original 2026 observed-country panel. The reproducibility output and source checksums are preserved in [run 40241769129](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40241769129), [artifact 12616508530](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40241769129/artifacts/12616508530). These are computational records, not third-party scientific peer review.

For every vintage `v`, economy `i` and fixed 2000–2019 endpoint pair, the country-level estimand is `g_iv = [ln(y_i,2019,v) − ln(y_i,2000,v)] / 19`. The fixed three-release cohort is the **intersection of positive observed endpoints in all three vintages (n = 155)**. For each release pair `(v,w)`, we compute `g_iw − g_iv`, its cross-economy median and the number of countries for which `sign(g_iw) != sign(g_iv)`. The median of country-level differences is **not** the difference of the two release medians. Every country has equal weight; these are not population- or worker-weighted results. As an exploratory margin-to-zero check, we also count a sign change only when **both** compared annualized log growth rates have absolute magnitude greater than each of 0.001, 0.002, 0.005 and 0.01 (0.1, 0.2, 0.5 and 1.0 log-percentage points/year). These thresholds were selected **after** inspecting the main results, are not prespecified inferential cutoffs, and should not be interpreted as statistical significance. There is no significance test, bootstrap uncertainty interval, causal design, or adjustment for dependence between releases. Source revisions cannot be attributed to either the national-accounts numerator or ILO employment denominator from this composite indicator alone.

The October 2024 archived series provides **no comparable 2023 endpoint observations** in the inspected extract. Consequently, the three-vintage 2000–2023 estimand is **not identifiable from these three releases**; the study does not fill the missing historical cells. This missingness is a restriction on the source comparison, not evidence of zero productivity.


### 2.5 Within-release horizon sensitivity as a falsification guard against vintage conflation

As a separate **post hoc descriptive sensitivity check**, we used only the version-controlled 2026 WDI observed-country CSV, with its 2000, 2019 and 2023 endpoint observations. We required strictly positive, finite values at all three dates for each economy, yielding the same balanced three-date cohort (n=153), but **not** the separate 155-economy three-vintage 2000–2019 cohort. For each economy we calculated annualised log growth over 2000–2019 and 2000–2023 and their **within-country paired difference**. We compared the median of paired differences with the difference of the two cross-country medians; these are distinct estimands. We additionally counted the sign of each paired difference, sign switches between the two horizon-specific growth rates, and the exact leave-one-economy-out range of the paired-difference median. This diagnostic does not compare WDI releases, infer a policy or productivity shock, estimate statistical confidence intervals, or independently observe agricultural employment. Reproduction: `analysis/ae_2026_fixed_balanced_horizon_sensitivity.py` and the original panel CSV, executed in [source-locked GitHub Actions run 40299175110](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40299175110) with [country-level artifact 12639591781](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40299175110/artifacts/12639591781). ### 2.6 Independent absolute agricultural employment level-comparison audit (not historical release attribution)

To check the algebraically inferred WDI denominator against an **external absolute-employment source**, we downloaded the ILO-maintained original `EMP_TEMP_SEX_ECO_NB_A` annual RDS (`https://rplumber.ilo.org/files/indicator/EMP_TEMP_SEX_ECO_NB_A.rds`), retaining **2,306,384 source bytes** and SHA256 `956f6d27cbf9a6cba969cf7ceb37b1715b40704c984fdcc76ea6aff2a48ae0cb` (source-locked [run 40299957865](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40299957865)). We restricted to `SEX_T`, **`ECO_AGGREGATE_AGR`** (broad agriculture), `best_source=1`, annual 2000 and 2019 records; we did **not** pool ISIC Rev.3 A/B components with Rev.4 A or count duplicated survey sources. This yielded a separate unambiguous absolute-employment country-year dataset ([coverage run 40300545351](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40300545351)). ILOSTAT values in **thousands of persons** were converted to persons by multiplying by 1,000.

We separately downloaded the **official WDI 2026** agricultural value-added numerator `NV.AGR.TOTL.KD` (constant 2015 US$; API last updated **2026-10-08**, source SHA256 `7df780de72f5cfa5951501f4be88b4a7fa112bd95f08814d3f5d844393a84323`) and joined it to the archived 2026 WDI per-worker indicator `NV.AGR.EMPL.KD` by exact ISO3 and year. The WDI-implied denominator `L*=V/P` is in **persons**, and the **separately retrieved** ILOSTAT level is compared through `ILOSTAT_persons/L*`, reporting absolute relative and absolute log discrepancies. We required positive finite numerator, denominator and independent worker counts; no missing values were imputed. This comparison uses **contemporary source releases** and only the intersecting countries; it **does not** retrieve matching historical ILOSTAT 2024 and 2025 releases, identify revisions' causes, or validate all 155 countries in the fixed historical-vintage sample. Reproducible country-year output: [run 40300738631](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40300738631), [artifact 12640371610](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40300738631/artifacts/12640371610).


### 2.7 Source-stability sensitivity of two-endpoint agricultural worker-growth agreement

Using the separately source-locked, matched country-year worker levels from Section 2.6, we retained economies with positive values from **both 2000 and 2019** in both data families. For each such economy, we computed `g_ILO = ln(ILOSTAT_workers_2019 / ILOSTAT_workers_2000)/19` and `g_WDI = ln(WDI_implied_workers_2019 / WDI_implied_workers_2000)/19`, their signed and absolute differences, and whether their growth signs disagreed. We separately described economies whose **ILOSTAT source code** stayed the same versus changed between endpoints, without equating a stable code with invariant survey design. This is a **current-source, within-economy growth-consistency sensitivity**, not a comparison of historical WDI or ILOSTAT releases, a causal decomposition, a formal significance test, or a fully independent labor-input measurement. The original country-level source-locked audit was executed in [run 40301314822](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40301314822), [artifact 12640686025](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40301314822/artifacts/12640686025).

### 2.8 Matched-country selection and country-level resampling uncertainty for worker-growth agreement

As an explicitly post hoc *diagnostic of the already reported matched cohort* rather than a new historical-vintage estimate, we cross-referenced the 73 two-endpoint ILOSTAT/WDI worker-growth comparisons with the separately archived 2026 WDI agricultural value-added-per-worker panel. The 2026 panel contains 157 positive 2000/2019 economy pairs; the matched ILOSTAT subset contains 73, leaving 84 without matched two-endpoint ILOSTAT worker-growth comparisons. We compared the annualised **WDI agricultural productivity** log-growth distributions for included versus excluded economies as a descriptive selection screen; productivity growth must not be confused with agricultural employment growth. For the 73 matched economies, we resampled **countries**, not country-years, with replacement for 6,000 deterministic iterations (seed 20261026), obtaining percentile 2.5%–97.5% ranges for the median absolute cross-source worker-growth discrepancy and the difference in subgroup medians (changed minus stable ILO source code). We also recomputed the median after omitting each country and assessed sign disagreement after excluding economies for which either worker-growth estimate lay within 0, 0.1, 0.25 or 0.5 log-percentage points/year of zero. These **conditional, exploratory resampling ranges are not probability-based confidence intervals for the world's economies**, do not correct nonrandom selection or dependence in underlying source construction, and do not establish source-change causation. Reproducible original-country results: [run 40309574730](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40309574730), [artifact 12643643751](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40309574730/artifacts/12643643751); input growth JSON SHA256 `3feee3649062338bc55489019791cbddde608714eb4ab7837dde486f710c28a8` and archived 2026 WDI panel SHA256 `715ff36b3b817dcf58a4170eadad872b0bd629bd66a424a73fac077dac869169`.

### 2.9 Regional composition of the matched absolute-worker comparison

We conducted an additional **post hoc** regional selection audit on the exact same **157** positive 2000/2019 economy pairs in the archived 2026 WDI country panel and the **73** economies with matched current-source ILOSTAT/WDI two-endpoint agricultural worker-growth records. Country regions came from the original dated WDI country metadata; they are analytical group labels, not random-sampling strata. We tabulated matched/unmatched counts and conditional coverage proportions within each region, without imputing missing employment. To quantify departure from region-independent matching **within this fixed observed panel**, we computed the Pearson two-column chi-square descriptive statistic and Cramer's V, then permuted the 73 matched labels uniformly across the 157 fixed countries **10,000** times with seed **20261026**, retaining the observed total matched count and region sizes. The Monte Carlo tail fraction used the +1 correction. This post hoc permutation is a **conditional exchangeability sensitivity**, not a probability-sample significance test for the world or proof of the missingness mechanism. Small regions (including a one-economy North America group) make extrapolation particularly inappropriate. The script `analysis/ae_ilostat_region_selection_bias_audit.py` verifies original SHA256-locked country-level inputs and retains no causal or publication-ready claim; [run 40317629788](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40317629788), [artifact 12647203753](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40317629788/artifacts/12647203753).

### 2.10 Additional 2019–2023 cohort-composition and influential-case sensitivity (post hoc)

To distinguish the 153-economy three-endpoint panel from the larger set eligible for the **2019–2023 interval alone**, we independently enumerated positive finite 2019/2023 pairs from the original archived official WDI country CSV. For each eligible economy, annualized log growth was calculated as `100 ln(P_2023/P_2019)/4` in log-percentage points per year. We compared country-equal-weighted means, medians and signs for the 153 three-endpoint economies, the 16 economies lacking a positive 2000 endpoint, and their 169-economy union. As an explicitly **post hoc** influence check, we recalculated the 169-economy summary excluding the two largest additional-cohort positive observations (CAF and DJI), without labeling either as erroneous. The fail-closed source-locked script `analysis/wdi_2019_2023_unbalanced_cohort_sensitivity.py` ran successfully in [GitHub Actions 40352237660](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40352237660), [artifact 12663744039](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40352237660/artifacts/12663744039). This is not a paired within-country treatment effect, historical-release attribution or a world-weighted growth estimate.

### 2.11 Exploratory regional decomposition of the 2019–2023 cohort mean (post hoc)

We decomposed the equal-economy mean growth difference between the 153-economy three-endpoint cohort and the 169-economy 2019/2023 cohort into changes in regional sample shares and changes in observed within-region country means. With baseline/pooled regional shares w_B,r and w_P,r and regional means m_B,r and m_P,r, the exact identity is: mean_P − mean_B = Σ_r[(w_P,r − w_B,r)m_B,r] + Σ_r[w_P,r(m_P,r − m_B,r)]. The baseline-reference decomposition is descriptive, not unique or causal. It was computed from the same source-hashed 217-economy WDI panel and seven official region labels; excluding CAF and DJI is a **post-hoc influence check only**. Full country source, code, formulas and region table are documented in [C16](AE-2026-001_C16_REGION_STANDARDIZED_COHORT_MEAN_DECOMPOSITION_2026-10-26.md). This is not a historical-vintage, representative-population or methodological-novelty claim.

### 2.12 Exact added-economy mean-gap attribution and single-deletion sensitivity (post hoc)

As an additional diagnostic of the 153-versus-169 economy selection contrast, we decomposed the equal-economy 2019–2023 mean log-growth gap into 16 baseline-relative contributions. Let B be the 153 economies observed in 2000, 2019 and 2023, and A the 16 with positive 2019/2023 values but no positive 2000 endpoint. For annual log growth g, the exact identity is mean(B∪A) − mean(B) = Σ_(i∈A)[g_i − mean(B)]/169. We also recomputed pooled means and medians after deleting **one added economy at a time** solely to describe influence; no observations were removed from the primary analysis. Calculations use the existing source-locked 217-economy official WDI CSV, and the 16-entry audit is documented in [C19](AE-2026-001_C19_EXACT_16_ADDED_ECONOMY_MEAN_ATTRIBUTION_2026-10-26.md). This is a descriptive, reference-dependent, post-hoc accounting identity, not a causal attribution or independent statistical innovation.

### 2.13 Numerical benchmark of CAGR versus log-growth conventions (source-locked, post hoc)

To separate differences in the *definition* of annual growth from differences in underlying WDI releases, we performed a direct numerical calculation using the documented `countryatlas::growth_rate(type="cagr")` compound-rate convention as a mathematical comparator. For the same positive country-level endpoints, ordinary CAGR is `100[(P_end/P_start)^(1/T)−1]` percent/year, whereas our primary outcome is `100 ln(P_end/P_start)/T` log-percentage points/year. Both were computed for the 157 economies with positive 2000/2019 endpoints and the 169 with positive 2019/2023 endpoints in the *same version-controlled 2026 WDI CSV*. We prespecified no exclusions beyond the existing positive-endpoint gate and reported differences in absolute growth magnitude, ranks and signs. The code verifies source SHA256 and cohort counts and emits all country-level values (C22 source-locked audit; successful GitHub Actions run 40389894089, artifact 12681099650). **This is a numerical implementation of the documented formula, not an execution of the countryatlas R package or a head-to-head `compare_vintages` analysis.**

### 2.14 Region-stratified contrast of non-overlapping annualized growth intervals (post hoc)

As a secondary regional heterogeneity diagnostic, we retained the same **153** economies with positive observed 2000, 2019 and 2023 values in the dated 2026 WDI source. For each economy, we subtracted its annualized **2000–2019** log growth from its annualized **2019–2023** log growth (non-overlapping 19-year and 4-year intervals). We calculated region-specific medians, direction counts and pooled medians after omitting each region in turn, using the existing WDI regional labels. The source-locked row-level audit C23 explicitly separates this contrast from the overlapping-horizon 2000–2019 versus 2000–2023 analysis and the 155-economy historical-vintage cohort. No inferential population weights or causal assumptions were introduced.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Data coverage

The official API returned 266 indicator records per selected year before excluding aggregate groups. Country metadata contained 296 entries; after aggregate exclusion, the analysis set contained **217 economies**. A positive value was available for **158 (72.81%)** in 2000, **173 (79.72%)** in 2019 and **169 (77.88%)** in 2023. Only **153 (70.51%)** had positive observations in all three years. Thus 64 of the 217 economy records could not contribute to a common three-year comparison. This coverage pattern is a descriptive property of the selected indicator and release; it is not evidence that excluded economies had zero production or zero productivity. **Exhaustive availability-mask census (C14):** in the order 2000/2019/2023, the 217 economies split into 43 with mask 000 (none), 16 with 011 (2019 and 2023 only), one with 100 (Yemen, 2000 only), four with 110 (2000 and 2019 only) and 153 with 111 (all three); the other three masks have zero observations. Thus all 169 economies with positive 2023 observations also have positive 2019 observations in this specific snapshot, but only 153 have a positive 2000 baseline. This pattern is descriptive source availability, not evidence of zero production or a causal explanation for missingness, and it is separate from the 155-economy historical-vintage cohort.

### 3.2 Period-specific versus balanced-sample summaries

For 2000–2019, **157** economies had valid endpoint pairs. Their median annualized log change was **0.023555752946417118** (approximately 2.36 log-percentage points per year), and the mean was **0.023984983654433946** (approximately 2.40). Restricting the same interval to the **153** economies with all three observations left the median unchanged at **0.023555752946417118**, while the mean became **0.024020706240511433** (approximately 2.40). This difference in mean is small in this snapshot but cannot be assumed to be small in other releases, indicators or country groupings. **Country-level attrition reconciliation (new source-file audit C13):** the four economies with valid 2000/2019 pairs but no positive 2023 observation in this dated extract are Sudan (SDN, 0.023310 log-pp/year, rank 27 of 157), Syrian Arab Republic (SYR, 0.074789, rank 28), West Bank and Gaza (PSE, 4.110956, rank 118) and Ukraine (UKR, 4.838383, rank 136). The two low-rank and two high-rank deletions leave Bulgaria (BGR) as the same central order statistic in both samples (rank 79/157 versus 77/153), explaining why the 2000–2019 median is exactly unchanged at 2.355575 log-pp/year; this is an arithmetic cancellation, **not** proof of ignorable missingness. These current-source 157/153 groups must not be conflated with the historical three-vintage 155-economy cohort. The full reproducible country-level record is in C13.

For 2000–2023, **153** economies had valid endpoint pairs, identical in count to the three-year balanced panel. The median annualized log change was **0.02224483704398673** (approximately 2.22 log-percentage points per year), and the mean was **0.020785340670841517** (approximately 2.08). Both values are descriptive rates computed from two endpoint observations, not annual growth observations averaged over the intervening years.

### 3.3 Regional coverage as an inferential warning

The 153-economy balanced sample contains **45** entries classified as Europe & Central Asia, **36** as Sub-Saharan Africa, **28** as Latin America & Caribbean, **22** as East Asia & Pacific, **15** as Middle East, North Africa, Afghanistan & Pakistan, **6** as South Asia and **1** as North America. These are metadata categories from the current WDI response, not stable country groupings across all historical years. In particular, a regional summary based on one North American country cannot represent the region. Regional median differences were not tested and must not be interpreted as structural or policy effects.

### 3.4 Robustness to country-region composition and extreme growth observations

To test whether an identical-country sample resolves the remaining composition problem, we conducted an additional independent calculation using the *archived actual WDI country CSV*, omitting one World Bank region at a time and recalculating both endpoints in the same remaining economies. The reproducible Python script is `analysis/wdi_geographic_robustness_from_archived_real_panel.py`; the full numerical record is `AE-2026-001_SECOND_PASS_REAL_PANEL_GEOGRAPHIC_ROBUSTNESS_AND_NOVELTY_VERDICT_2026-10-25.md`. This is an explicitly **descriptive sensitivity exercise**, not an effect estimate for any region, significance test or exogenous natural experiment.

With all 153 complete cases the median annualized log productivity change was **0.023556** in 2000–2019 and **0.022245** in 2000–2023. Excluding the **45** Europe & Central Asia economies reduced the common-panel sample to 108 and the corresponding medians to **0.017299** and **0.015483**. Excluding **28** Latin America & Caribbean economies instead left 125 with medians **0.027116** and **0.023946**. Excluding 36 Sub-Saharan African economies left 117, with medians **0.027119** and **0.024433**. These comparisons reveal materially different unweighted-country composition; they cannot be used to attribute economic growth to inclusion of one region. As the 2000-based intervals overlap, their annual-rate difference is not an isolated change over 2019–2023.

As an exploratory outlier-robustness comparison, trimming the top and bottom seven annual rates from the 153 balanced countries (5% from each end, 139 retained observations) changed the 2000–2019 **mean** from **0.024021 to 0.024141** and the 2000–2023 mean from **0.020785 to 0.021260**; medians remain **0.023556** and **0.022245** under that symmetric trimming. These results are not confidence intervals. Importantly, the displayed raw panel includes only **one** complete-country observation in the World Bank's North America category, so no sample statistic here should be labeled a representative estimate for that region. The concept remains value added per worker (not USDA's total-factor-productivity index) and the denominator is the number of **economies**, not farm workers.

### 3.5 Implications of adversarial literature comparison

Our exploratory median rates are neither a discovery of international agricultural labor-productivity trends nor evidence of economic convergence. Dieppe and Matsuoka (2025, DOI **10.1007/s00181-024-02692-y**) already study agricultural contributions to productivity convergence using an independently constructed sectoral panel. Inklaar and Marapin (2025, STEG WP121) analyze sectoral convergence and labor reallocation using sectoral purchasing-power adjustments. Lagakos and Waugh (2013, DOI **10.1257/aer.103.2.948**) establish that sectoral worker selection can complicate aggregate agriculture-productivity comparisons. Therefore, a newly downloaded three-date WDI panel and standard leave-one-region-out analysis do **not alone establish journal-level research novelty**. A potential, much narrower contribution would be reproducible auditing of geographical coverage vulnerability in a version-dated public indicator. Publication as an original Research article needs independent validation against comparable audits and an author-led question with sufficiently new inference.

The median discrepancy for 13 economies with changed ILOSTAT source codes exceeded that of 60 with stable codes by **0.4566 log-percentage points/year**; its exploratory within-group country-bootstrap percentile range was **0.2463–0.8578 log-percentage points/year**. This nonrandom 13-economy subgroup is too small and heterogeneous for causal source-change attribution or global inference.

The **6/73** cross-source growth-sign disagreements fall to **3/68** when requiring both estimated annual worker-growth magnitudes to exceed **0.1 log-percentage points**, remain **3/65** at **0.25 log-percentage points**, and fall to **2/58** at **0.5 log-percentage points**. This shows that some cross-source sign disagreements are close to zero. They must not be confused with the **6/6/10 historical WDI release-induced sign flips** among the separate 155-economy cohort. All statistics were computed on observed countries and current source releases; neither the bootstrap ranges nor the cohort comparison identifies historical ILOSTAT vintages or a new general estimator. [Original source-locked run 40309574730](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40309574730), [artifact 12643643751](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40309574730/artifacts/12643643751).

### 3.18 Regional concentration of current-source employment coverage

Of the **157** WDI-positive 2000/2019 economies, **73** had the matched two-endpoint current-source ILOSTAT/WDI agricultural worker-growth comparison. Coverage was uneven across WDI regions: **32/46 (69.6%)** in Europe and Central Asia, **16/28 (57.1%)** in Latin America and the Caribbean, **12/22 (54.5%)** in East Asia and the Pacific, **6/17 (35.3%)** in the Middle East/North Africa/Afghanistan/Pakistan region, **2/6 (33.3%)** in South Asia, and **4/37 (10.8%)** in Sub-Saharan Africa. The remaining North America group contained one matched economy (1/1) and should not be interpreted as a regional coverage rate for a population. The fixed-panel region-by-matching diagnostic gave **Pearson chi-square = 33.0553** and **Cramer's V = 0.45885**; none of 10,000 uniformly permuted 73-country labels reached the observed statistic, giving the finite-simulation conditional tail estimate **p = 1/10001 ≈ 0.00010**. This is evidence of a strong **within-panel descriptive association** between region and availability of the matched cross-source comparison, **not** design-based significance, causal identification or generalization to all countries. A subsequent **post-hoc arithmetic decomposition of the seven-region contingency table** shows that Sub-Saharan Africa accounts for **18.9406/33.0553 = 57.3%** of the observed Pearson statistic (4 matched versus 17.20 expected within this fixed panel); Europe/Central Asia accounts for another **29.8%** (32 versus 21.39 expected). Their within-panel matched-coverage ratio is **6.43**, not a population risk ratio. Sparse expected counts, including both cells of the one-economy North America row, make a conventional asymptotic chi-square p-value unreliable; the reported permutation result is conditional and exploratory. Excluding the singleton North America row leaves **72/156 matched economies**, with a six-region **chi-square of 31.9296** and **Cramer's V = 0.4524**, close to the full-panel 0.45885; the concentration pattern does not depend on that one economy, though the remaining sparse groups still preclude population-level inference. These figures are derived from the already source-locked regional counts, **not new primary observations** ([C11 concentration audit](AE-2026-001_C11_REGIONAL_COVERAGE_CHI_SQUARE_CONTRIBUTION_AUDIT_2026-10-26.md)). The underrepresentation of Sub-Saharan African economies materially restricts interpretation of the 73-country source-agreement results. [Source-locked run 40317629788](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40317629788), [artifact 12647203753](https://github.com/mojtabarozegar11-ship-it/Saeit/actions/runs/40317629788/artifacts/12647203753).

### 3.19 Leave-one-region-out influence on matched employment coverage (post hoc)

A new **secondary sensitivity calculation** removed each WDI region from the previously source-locked 157-economy matched-coverage contingency table and recomputed the region-by-availability association using the remaining economies. The full seven-region Cramer's V was **0.4588**. Removing **Sub-Saharan Africa** left **69/120** economies matched and reduced V to **0.2649** (Pearson chi-square **8.4235**), whereas removing any other single region gave V between **0.4291 and 0.4963**. In the original fixed panel, matched coverage was **4/37 (10.81%)** in Sub-Saharan Africa and **69/120 (57.50%)** elsewhere; the descriptive coverage ratio was **0.1880** and odds ratio **0.08959**. These are **within-panel contrasts**, not population risk estimates. Because each leave-one-region-out table changes the country set, its chi-square statistic is not directly comparable to the full-table statistic as if N were fixed. The calculations are derived entirely from the earlier regional counts and provide **no new primary observations, random sampling design, historical ILOSTAT vintage, causal missingness explanation or methodological priority**. Full region-by-region arithmetic and executable standard-library reproduction are in [C12](AE-2026-001_C12_LEAVE_ONE_REGION_OUT_MATCHED_COVERAGE_SENSITIVITY_2026-10-26.md).

### 3.20 Unbalanced 2019–2023 endpoint cohort and high-leverage sensitivity (post hoc)

The dated official panel contains **153** economies with positive 2000, 2019 and 2023 observations and an additional **16** with positive 2019/2023 observations but no positive 2000 baseline. The 2019–2023 annualized log-growth **median** is **0.890440 log-pp/year** in the balanced 153 and **0.975110** in the 169-economy eligible set, a **+0.084671** difference of sample medians. The corresponding means are **0.541735** and **0.810142**, a **+0.268407** difference. The additional 16 alone have median **2.673188**, mean **3.376783**, and **11 positive / 5 negative** rates. They include seven economies classified as Sub-Saharan Africa, five as Middle East/North Africa/Afghanistan/Pakistan, two as Europe/Central Asia and two as East Asia/Pacific in this dated metadata.

Two additional-cohort observations are particularly influential: Central African Republic (**+28.1538**) and Djibouti (**+17.2598** log-pp/year). Excluding just those two **after seeing the results** yields a 167-economy mean of **0.547907** (only **+0.006171** above the balanced-cohort mean) and median **0.890440**. This is a sensitivity calculation, **not** grounds to delete these valid-source observations or a claim that their reported changes are caused by specific events. The 153-, 157-, 169- and historical fixed 155-economy denominators answer different questions and must not be interchanged. All results and source hashes are retained in [C15](AE-2026-001_C15_UNBALANCED_2019_2023_ENDPOINT_COHORT_SENSITIVITY_2026-10-26.md). This composition result is descriptive, not a new estimator or a validation of archived ILOSTAT employment vintages.

### 3.21 Regional composition and within-region contributions to the 2019–2023 mean gap (post hoc)

The equal-economy mean annual log growth was **+0.541735** in the 153-economy baseline and **+0.810142** in the 169-economy eligible cohort, a gap of **+0.268407 log-percentage points/year**. The exact baseline-region identity decomposed the gap into **−0.044876** due to regional sample-share reweighting and **+0.313283** due to within-region sample-mean replacement. The largest positive within-region contributions were **+0.184244** for the Middle East/North Africa/Afghanistan/Pakistan region and **+0.179274** for Sub-Saharan Africa; East Asia/Pacific contributed **−0.098244**. In a strictly post-hoc diagnostic excluding CAF and DJI from the pooled group only, the mean gap fell to **+0.006171** (composition **−0.032963**, within-region **+0.039134**). These are algebraic components of **sample selection**, not region effects, causal drivers, WDI vintage revisions, a new estimator or a rationale to discard influential observations. The full reproducible seven-region decomposition appears in [C16](AE-2026-001_C16_REGION_STANDARDIZED_COHORT_MEAN_DECOMPOSITION_2026-10-26.md).

### 3.22 Exact contributions of the 16 additional economies (post hoc)

The 153-economy mean 2019–2023 growth of **+0.5417354** increased to **+0.8101423** log-percentage points/year for the 169-economy sample, a gap of **+0.2684069**. Under the baseline-relative identity in Section 2.12, the added Central African Republic observation contributed **+0.1633850** and Djibouti **+0.0989237** log-pp/year to this *arithmetic* gap. Removing CAF alone from the 169-economy set yields a mean of **+0.6473825**; removing DJI alone yields **+0.7122275**, both **post hoc**. ### 3.23 Formula-equivalent countryatlas CAGR comparison: scale changes, not vintage changes

For 2000–2019, the 157-economy median was **2.355575 log-percentage points/year** under our natural-log convention versus **2.383538 percent/year** under ordinary CAGR. For 2019–2023, the corresponding 169-economy medians were **0.975110** and **0.979880**. Absolute country-level differences exceeded 0.1 percentage points/year for **32/157** and **55/169** economies, respectively, and exceeded 0.5 for **0/157** and **10/169**. The largest conversion gaps were **Slovakia +0.490912** (2000–2019) and **Malta +6.893831** (2019–2023), with Malta's log growth **−39.579082** versus CAGR **−32.685251**. These differences arise with **unchanged observations from a single WDI release** and therefore must not be counted as historical revisions. ### 3.24 Regional heterogeneity of non-overlapping period contrasts (post hoc)

Across the **153** complete-endpoint economies, **109** had lower annualized growth in 2019–2023 than in 2000–2019 and **44** had higher growth. ## 4. Discussion

The principal result is methodological modesty rather than a newly discovered economic law. A widely used official indicator is missing for a material fraction of the nominal country set in all three selected years. A common-country restriction produces a reproducible descriptive comparison, but does not eliminate missing-not-at-random selection. The observed median difference between 2000–2019 and 2000–2023 also combines different end years and time intervals, including economic and environmental events after 2019, so it cannot be assigned exclusively to coverage changes. Indeed, the 2000–2023 sample coincides with the three-year balanced set here; this specific sample provides little evidence of large median sensitivity to the balanced restriction.

The additional rank-stability audit finds 26 of 155 economies with more than one log-percentage point per year of historical-growth revision between the 2024 and 2026 releases, despite a Spearman rank correlation of 0.887. This supports describing release uncertainty with both ranking and magnitude statistics, while leaving the source of revisions unidentified.

Country-level value added per worker may rise when measured agricultural employment falls, even if output, working hours, land productivity or household income follow different trajectories. Comparisons may also be affected by ILO modeled employment, the treatment of informal or family labor, re-benchmarking of national accounts, sector reclassification, currency conversion and different availability of historical estimates. The source-locked 2024–2025 auxiliary value-added series and implied-denominator identity in section 3.13 remain a **noncausal algebraic decomposition**. Section 3.15 now adds a genuine independent ILOSTAT **absolute-employment level comparison**, but only for a selected 2026-release intersection, not for matching historical ILOSTAT vintages or all 155 economies. Its 5.81% median absolute relative discrepancy and the additional 0.4104 log-percentage-point median absolute annual worker-growth discrepancy among 73 two-endpoint economies are descriptive source-consistency measures, **not** proof that employment revisions caused the WDI productivity revisions. A stronger historical mechanism study still requires separately archived 2024/2025 ILOSTAT releases, comparable source definitions, explicit sample attrition and competing prior-art benchmarks. Section 3.17 quantifies that attrition directly: the 73 matched countries have higher median WDI agricultural productivity growth than the 84 unmatched countries, so worker-growth agreement statistics should not be extrapolated to the 155-economy historical-vintage sample. Its country-level bootstrap is a conditional stability diagnostic, not design-based population uncertainty. The new Section 3.18 shows that the matched-country sample is also regionally concentrated (69.6% Europe/Central Asia versus 10.8% Sub-Saharan Africa), strengthening the warning against treating 73 current-source matched economies as a representative validation of the historical 155-economy cohort.

Prior research provides both substantive and methodological reasons for restraint. Gutierrez (2002) and Dieppe and Matsuoka (2021/2025) already discuss agricultural labor-productivity patterns and convergence; Eberhardt and Teal (2013) show why cross-country sector heterogeneity matters; and Gollin and colleagues highlight the sensitivity of aggregate productivity gaps to labor-input measurement. The current work must therefore not be marketed as a first global analysis or a novel convergence result. **Direct methodological prior art also constrains the revision-sensitivity claim.** Johnson, Larson, Papageorgiou and Subramanian (2013) demonstrated that successive Penn World Table releases can alter cross-country growth estimates, with stronger instability at higher frequencies; their study concerns a different GDP dataset and does not establish this WDI agricultural indicator's revisions [10]. Filer, Hájková and Hanousek (2004) showed that data choice can alter replicated cross-country growth-determinant conclusions [11]. Wolff, Chong and Auffhammer (2011) documented how source updates and methodological changes can alter country classifications in the Human Development Index [12]. These are genuine predecessors to the **general phenomenon** of release-sensitive growth and country classification. Consequently, the present three-vintage fixed-cohort audit cannot claim invention of vintage sensitivity, sign reclassification, or a general data-revision methodology. Its bounded candidate contribution is the documented magnitude and non-monotone sign trajectory for **NV.AGR.EMPL.KD** in these specific archived releases; the relevance of that indicator-specific case still requires editorial and human scholarly judgment. The additional **fixed-country 2024/2025/2026 vintage audit** now quantifies observed sign-classification instability on an identical cohort. The World Bank explicitly documents historical WDI revisions, and the USDA Economic Research Service also documents revisions to its **different** agricultural total-factor-productivity database; neither source proves that this specific NV.AGR.EMPL.KD fixed-cohort calculation is a first publication. Its potential contribution is a release-dated, falsifiable **data-quality research note**, conditional on a deeper novelty comparison and human scientific authorship.

**Direct WDI software prior art (independently checked 26 October 2026).** The publicly documented R package *countryatlas*, function `compare_vintages(indicator, vintages, year, countries)`, already provides same-indicator WDI comparisons across historical releases, matched-country revisions, median absolute revision summaries and explicit warnings when release-specific indicator units change. Its public reference is https://pursuitofdatascience.github.io/countryatlas/reference/compare_vintages.html and source is https://github.com/PursuitOfDataScience/countryatlas/blob/main/R/worldbank.R . This documentation was checked as **current methodological prior art**, not as proof of the package's date of first implementation or of an earlier peer-reviewed article. The present work therefore cannot claim a new ability to compare WDI vintages, detect revisions, or guard against unit rebasing. Its potentially useful contribution is restricted to the transparent **2000–2019, 155-economy, three-release case study for NV.AGR.EMPL.KD**, its annualized growth sign/rank/magnitude consequences and source-hashed release files. A direct **source-code inspection** of the upstream R function (internal audit C7, 26 October 2026) confirms that this particular function computes **single-year level revisions** relative to a baseline vintage; it does not itself compute two-endpoint annualised log-growth revisions, growth-sign flips or growth-rank correlations. Those AE outputs are standard downstream transformations of archived data, **not** a new estimator or proof of first-publication priority. No matched numerical execution of countryatlas against the identical Excel/API source snapshots was performed, and novelty remains **unproven**.

## 5. Limitations

The exploratory C19 country-level mean attribution is an **exact accounting identity**, not a causal contribution or a decomposition of population-wide variance. Its 97.73% two-country figure depends on the 153-economy baseline reference and the cancellation of other signed terms; it is not an invariant share of an economic effect. The single-deletion diagnostics were selected after observing the data and do not authorize dropping CAF or DJI. No new 2024/2025 ILOSTAT archives or numerical countryatlas benchmark were obtained ([C19](AE-2026-001_C19_EXACT_16_ADDED_ECONOMY_MEAN_ATTRIBUTION_2026-10-26.md)).


The post-hoc leave-one-region-out coverage audit shows that the descriptive association is strongly influenced by Sub-Saharan Africa's low availability; removing that region reduces Cramer's V from 0.4588 to 0.2649, but does not identify a missingness mechanism or recover the unobserved countries. Regional coverage comparisons use the 157-economy current-source eligibility panel, **not** the 155-economy historical-vintage fixed cohort. The sensitivity results are not probability-sample inference or a correction for selection bias ([C12](AE-2026-001_C12_LEAVE_ONE_REGION_OUT_MATCHED_COVERAGE_SENSITIVITY_2026-10-26.md)).



The primary analysis examines three endpoint years of one public per-worker series and **does reconstruct its published values from two original historical WDI Excel releases plus one 2026 API snapshot**; the auxiliary 2024–2025 value-added numerator audit in section 3.13 is an algebraic consistency check, not independent ILO labor measurement. It does **not** reconstruct the underlying national-accounts or ILO employment revision mechanisms, assess uncertainty in ILO employment estimates, inspect original national data, model dependence across economies, identify causal determinants or distinguish output-per-hour from output-per-worker. The balanced panel is selected on data availability, potentially inducing selection bias. Equal-country-weighted statistics are not global worker-weighted statistics. No design-based population uncertainty is identified; the Section 3.17 country-level bootstrap is only an exploratory conditional resampling sensitivity for a nonrandom matched subset, and regional subsamples can be small or unrepresentative. The report is an initial empirical manuscript draft and its publication-level novelty has not been established.

The newly downloaded ILOSTAT source has absolute employment observations retrieved independently of the WDI files, but **the underlying labor estimates may not be statistically independent of WDI**, since WDI cites ILOSTAT as an input. Moreover, the 200 matched country-year records (127 economies) reflect nonrandom availability, possible source/classification mismatches and repeated countries across two years; no probability-based population sampling interval or release-vintage matching is available. The new 73-versus-84 country attrition audit and country-level bootstrap quantify selection and conditional instability without removing them. The source-agreement statistic is not a causal decomposition or a test of independent 2024/2025 employment revisions. The region-by-availability permutation in Section 3.18 conditions on a nonrandom country panel, was specified post hoc, includes sparse region groups and cannot establish population-level sampling significance or a causal reason for missing employment comparisons. A post-hoc decomposition (C11) shows that the observed Pearson association is driven primarily by the Sub-Saharan Africa coverage deficit, but small expected cell counts preclude treating the usual asymptotic chi-square tail probability as valid.

## 6. Conclusions

The official WDI snapshot contains 217 nonaggregate economies, but only 153 have positive agricultural value-added-per-worker observations for all three selected years. Descriptive median annualized log changes are positive for both 2000–2019 and 2000–2023; their interpretation is limited to the countries with observed endpoints. A fixed-country **three-vintage** comparison is feasible and reproducible for 2000–2019 (155 economies), showing 6, 6 and 10 growth-sign changes across consecutive and end-to-end release pairs; 2000–2023 is not three-vintage estimable because the 2024 source lacks comparable 2023 observations. These source-revision findings neither fix measurement heterogeneity nor establish convergence, causality or changes in farmer welfare. Any publication claim requires further sensitivity analyses, independent literature appraisal and substantive review by the accountable human author.
