# From Irrigation Recommendations to Field Outcomes: An Evidence-Provenance Critical Narrative Review

**Article type:** Critical narrative review of eight purposively selected studies. The reporting protocol is proposed and unvalidated; this is not a systematic review, a causal trial, or a meta-analysis. Source discrepancies are stated rather than silently repaired.

## Abstract

Irrigation decision-support systems (DSS) can generate agronomically plausible advice without necessarily changing farmers' actions or producing independently attributable water savings. This critical narrative review examines eight purposively selected published examples spanning farm-scale comparisons, research-station plots, advisory messaging, farmer-reported use, and virtual decision exercises. We distinguish physical measurements from reported perceptions, observed implementation, and model-based counterfactuals, while retaining the farm, plot, person, irrigation event and season as non-interchangeable units of analysis. The reviewed reports include measured irrigation contrasts, daily digital advice, rainfall-input reporting, stated next-season interest, participation records and simulated benefits; none of these evidence types alone establishes sustained voluntary adoption or causal conservation. Several numerical claims require particular caution: Car et al. (2012)'s 45/13/14 engagement-duration partition reconciles within the institutional abstract but not with the conflicting 74-count Table 5 classification; Amini et al. (2025) report inconsistent F7 irrigation-input water-productivity differences, while its source equation does not measure consumptive water use; and the 2020 Cvejić survey denominator cannot be established merely from its 35 implementation farms. Earlier studies already address adoption, advice-versus-applied irrigation, telemetry and grower comparisons. Accordingly, the possible incremental contribution of this review is **transparent source-to-claim adjudication**, not priority for studying adoption or a newly validated compliance measure. We propose an explicitly unvalidated reporting template linking recommendation, delivery, independently documented action, field outcome, comparator and later voluntary use. Missing full methods, heterogeneous designs and author verification preclude a pooled effect estimate or general prevalence claim.

**Keywords:** irrigation decision support; evidence provenance; farmer uptake; implementation fidelity; water productivity; narrative review.

## 1. Introduction

Technical validity, behavioral implementation and hydrologic outcomes are distinct propositions. A model may accurately predict crop water demand; an advisory service may reach a phone; a participant may report that advice is useful. None of these observations establishes that a specified irrigation recommendation was implemented, that applied volume was independently measured, or that an observed difference is attributable to the advice rather than weather, water allocation, crop choice or other changes. A rigorous synthesis must specify the observation and its denominator before translating an outcome into a claim.

The literature already addresses these distinctions. Ara et al. (2021) review **economic, tactical and strategic DSS** and distinguish software use from the adoption of decision heuristics; their Methods explicitly **exclude daily or real-time irrigation scheduling DSS**, so they cannot be cited as direct validation of an irrigation-event fidelity protocol. More direct prior art includes Lorite et al. (2012), who compare applied seasonal irrigation with advisory recommendations, Lorite et al. (2013), who report field telemetry in the same named district, and Baroni et al. (2019), who contrast six scheduling methods with end-user interviews. Elia et al. (2022) report daily smartphone advice and commercial-farm comparisons. Berthold et al. (2021) study barriers to irrigation-scheduling-tool adoption through mailed questionnaires; their 77 usable responses are not objective advice-event adherence observations. These precedents rule out any claim that this manuscript first identified the adoption gap, field observation, mobile delivery or farmer-versus-recommendation comparison. [Ara original publisher article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agwat.2021.107161); [Berthold original publisher article](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.atech.2021.100016).

Additional prior reviews further narrow the potential contribution: Mabhaudhi et al. (2023) map **81 agricultural-water-productivity decision-support tools** (tools, not growers or advice events); Bounajra et al. (2024) synthesize **55 field-scale technology studies** (studies, not implemented irrigation events); Wei et al. (2024) discuss human-centered AI, trust and social adoption; and Ofosu-Ampong et al. (2025) categorize **19 digital agro-advisory platforms** using subscriber/adopter bands (platform reach, not objectively observed farm action). None of these publication-level counts is an event-adherence denominator. These sources establish extensive existing adoption, technological and human-centered prior art; the manuscript cannot claim first-in-field conceptual novelty. [Mabhaudhi](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agwat.2023.108590); [Bounajra](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agwat.2024.108758); [Wei](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44230-024-00072-4); [Ofosu-Ampong](https://doi.org/10.1007/s44279-025-00190-y).


Two later *Scientific Reports* publications by Emami and Dehghanisanij overlap the irrigation-DSS and water-productivity landscape: Emami, Dehghanisanij and Emami (published **6 April 2026**, version of record **27 May 2026**) report an intelligent irrigation-timing/depth system for wheat, tomato and apple in the Miandoab Plain; Emami and Dehghanisanij (published **26 September 2026**) describe a process-based water-allocation framework across Lake Urmia and Alborz, three crops and 100%/80%/60% irrigation scenarios. Both concern agronomic/model decision support, **not a verified recommendation-delivery-to-physical-action denominator**. Their shared authors and overlapping geographic/crop context mean they must **not** be counted as two independent replications or merged with Amini (2025) as distinct experimental farm samples without raw design and provenance checks. These sources further prohibit novelty claims for irrigation timing/depth, crop-sensitive allocation or DSS water productivity; they do **not** resolve Amini F7. [Emami et al. 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-46523-9); [Emami and Dehghanisanij 2026](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-72760-z).

The narrow editorial question is instead: **what evidence supports each specific claim about a recommendation, an action and a water outcome, and where does the inferential chain break?** The aim is to illustrate how source-level provenance and explicit missingness can improve the interpretation of heterogeneous studies. Whether this approach adds enough scholarly value beyond existing reviews remains for the intended human author and an independent journal review to judge.


Fan and McCann (2020) used multilevel models with the US **2013 Farm and Ranch Irrigation Survey** to distinguish the adoption of pressure irrigation *systems* from adoption of *scientific irrigation scheduling practices*. Their published journal abstract associates the former more strongly with state-level variation and the latter with farm-level factors, with cost, management time, and time horizon among reported barriers. These are survey-model associations, not randomized causal estimates, observed recommendation-specific actions, or measured water savings. Consequently, hardware adoption, scheduling-practice adoption, message engagement and event-level compliance must be coded as **four separate constructs**. This published antecedent further narrows any novelty claim for adoption-factor analysis; the review's proposed claim-level provenance template is editorial and unvalidated. [Original journal article](https://jareonline.org/articles/adoption-of-pressure-irrigation-systems-and-scientific-irrigation-scheduling-practices-by-u-s-farmers-an-application-of-multilevel-models/) (Fan & McCann, 2020, *Journal of Agricultural and Resource Economics* 45(2), 352–375; [DOI](https://doi.org/10.22004/ag.econ.302459)).

## 2. Methods: scope, selection and coding

This is a **non-systematic critical narrative review**. Eight studies were selected purposively from documented bibliographic discovery and subsequent targeted source checking because they illustrate different combinations of field measurement, farmer reporting, advisory delivery and modeled scenarios. The set is neither an exhaustive sampling frame nor a denominator for estimating global DSS adoption or effectiveness. Earlier repository retrieval counts (672 bibliographic entries and 604 machine-matched candidate citations) are discovery-process records, **not PRISMA screening counts or a completed systematic search**. No independent dual-human screening, protocol registration or meta-analysis is asserted.

**Case-selection rationale (post hoc, not a prospective search protocol).** The eight cases are an editorially purposive set selected to expose distinct inference breaks, not a sample with known inclusion probabilities. Their roles in the argument are specified below so a reader can challenge their selection; this table does **not** establish that these were prespecified eligibility rules or that all eligible literature was found.

| Included case | Distinct inferential contrast motivating inclusion | Limitation retained |
|---|---|---|
| Amini (2025) | Within-farm DSS-guided versus customary sections | F7 inconsistency; nonrandom farm-level inference |
| Cvejić (2020) | Advisory implementation and dated respondent feedback | Recruitment, implementation and item-response frames unresolved |
| Jamal (2023) | Field observations alongside virtual farmer decisions | Virtual choices are not applied irrigation |
| Abi Saab (2019) | Replicated research-station water-depth comparisons | Plots do not establish voluntary commercial adoption |
| Car (2012) | SMS delivery, engagement and perceived decision influence | Source tables and item denominators conflict |
| Elia (2022) | Commercial-farm seasonal irrigation and N contrasts | Full allocation and action-linkage methods unverified |
| Baroni (2019) | Six method predictions versus qualitative end-user uptake | Six methods are not six farmer compliance observations |
| Darouich (2024) | Reported farmer schedules versus optimized counterfactuals | Missing entries and modeled outcomes limit inference |

Lorite's published advisory-versus-applied seasonal comparison and telemetry work, and Ara's adoption review, are discussed as **contextual prior art**, not silently counted among the eight. This editorial distinction is not evidence of their lesser scientific quality. The manuscript offers no PRISMA flow, exhaustive exclusion list, dual-screening record, search cutoff reproducibility or pooled effect estimate; these absences constrain generalization and must be disclosed in any journal submission. As an additional *editorial preparation aid*, the published SANRA framework (Baethge et al., 2019) identifies six narrative-review appraisal domains: importance, aims, literature search, referencing, scientific reasoning and appropriate endpoint data. SANRA was developed/tested in a medical editorial setting and is **not** a journal acceptance rule, author certification, independent reviewer verdict or a validated score for this agriculture manuscript. No SANRA score or external reviewer rating has been assigned. [SANRA source](https://doi.org/10.1186/s41073-019-0064-8).

For each example, we distinguish **M**: directly reported physical field measurement; **O**: independently documented implementation of a particular recommendation; **S**: self-report, survey or participant appraisal; **P**: prediction, optimization or simulated counterfactual; and **U**: an unresolved or insufficiently accessible source component. These are descriptive provenance labels, **not a validated ordinal quality score**. A study can contain several classes; a measured input used to validate a model does not turn the model's hypothetical output into a realized effect.

The unit of inference is recorded as person, farm, plot, irrigation event, crop-season or simulated decision. Where original full methods are unavailable, absence of evidence in the inspected abstract must not be presented as proof that the original investigators never collected that evidence. Numerical contradictions are flagged rather than repaired through speculative arithmetic. The intended human author has **not yet finalized study selection, source interpretation, conflict-of-interest disclosures or the final manuscript**.

## 3. Eight illustrative cases

### 3.1 Amini et al. (2025): within-farm comparison and unresolved F7 reporting

The original open-access article describes eight monitored farms/orchards selected from 80 candidate farms in four villages in the Lake Urmia basin, with customary and DSS-guided sections. Water delivery and yield were assessed at the section level and farmers participated in workshops. This is a real on-farm agronomic comparison, not 80 independently randomized treatment replicates. The source's F7 farm is displayed in **Figure 12**, not Figure 7 (whose caption depicts F3). **Table 4** (the measured irrigation-depth and water-productivity table) displays F7 water productivity rounded to one decimal as **4.6 versus 6.0 kg/m³**; subtracting the displayed values gives **−1.4 kg/m³**. However, under conventional nearest-tenth rounding the underlying difference could lie approximately between **−1.50 and −1.30 kg/m³**, so the Results' **−1.3 kg/m³** may be compatible with unrounded measurements and is **not, by itself, a demonstrated error**. The conclusion's **−0.1 kg/m³** lies outside that rounding interval and remains unexplained absent an alternative definition, correction or underlying observations. No single exact F7 effect is inferred from rounded values. **Source-location correction:** the original publisher Results first reports an F7 decline of **1.3 kg/m³**, its later Table 5 discussion explicitly reports **1.4 kg/m³**, and its Conclusion instead reports **0.1 kg/m³**. These are three *published statements*, not three independently estimated effects. The 1.4 statement agrees with the subtraction of the displayed one-decimal values; 1.3 could arise from undisclosed unrounded values; 0.1 is not explained by ordinary one-decimal rounding. The article says the underlying datasets are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request; those unrounded F7 observations and any publisher correction were **not** obtained. [Original publisher Results/Table 5/Conclusion and Data availability](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-26567-z). The treatment received **292.7 mm more** irrigation for F7 (**1057.6−764.9 mm** in Table 4); this is a water-depth contrast, not a direct measure of adherence. These discrepancies prevent a confidently reconstructed F7 effect and any pooled estimate from the rounded publication tables. The authors' participatory observations do not demonstrate later unsupported voluntary use.

**Original-source percent-column adjudication (30 October 2026).** A further cross-check of Amini et al.'s original **Table 5** finds that its column headed “Increase in water productivity (%)” prints **−3.22** for F7 and **+3.87** for F5. However, **Table 4** prints irrigation-input WP **4.6 versus 6.0 kg/m³** (F7) and **8.4 versus 4.6 kg/m³** (F5), treatment versus control. Conventional relative changes computed from those displayed values are **−23.33%** (F7) and **+82.61%** (F5), not the Table 5 column values. Even allowing nearest-tenth rounding, the F7 relative change is bounded approximately between **−24.79% and −21.85%** and F5 between **+79.57% and +85.71%**. The published percentage-column values are therefore **not identifiable as conventional treatment-versus-control relative changes in the displayed Table 4 WP**; an undisclosed alternative definition or source error remains possible. These calculations adjudicate the *arithmetic interpretation*, **not** the correct unpublished F7 effect, the reported significance test, or causality. **Source-text unit reconciliation for F5:** The original Amini Results describe the F5 increase as approximately **3.8 kg/m³** of water productivity, consistent with subtraction of the rounded Table 4 values (**8.4 − 4.6 = 3.8 kg/m³**). Yet Table 5 prints **+3.87** under a column labeled **(%)**. Its magnitude is compatible with an unrounded *absolute* difference in kg/m³, but that interpretation is not confirmed by source data and it is **not** a verified **+3.87%** change. F7 remains independently contradictory (**−1.3/−1.4/−0.1 kg/m³** in different source passages, and **−3.22** in the percentage-labeled column). The Table 5 column must not be pooled as percentage change without source-author clarification. [Original publisher Results and Tables 4–5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-26567-z). The source's Table 5 printed percentages must not be substituted for recomputed percentage effects or silently relabeled as kg/m³. [Original publisher, Tables 4–5](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-26567-z).

**C128 cross-level Amini source adjudication (30 October 2026).** The *original publisher abstract* reports a **3.87%** water-productivity increase for **drip systems**, while the body describes the F5 drip orchard's approximately **3.8 kg/m³** absolute increase and Table 5 prints **+3.87** under a percentage-labeled column. The same digits appearing at different levels (system-level abstract versus farm-level table) do **not** establish a common numerator, weighting, or unit. The aggregate 3.87% claim cannot be reconstructed from the displayed F5 treatment/control WP (8.4/4.6 kg/m³: a conventional rounded-value relative change of approximately +82.61%). Therefore the abstract's drip-system percentage is retained solely as an **author-reported aggregate**, not a verified F5 percentage or independently computed treatment effect; the farm-specific unit and denominator remain unresolved. [Original publisher abstract, Tables 4–5 and Results](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-26567-z).

Amini et al.'s Methods, “Collecting data on water flow” and Eq. (3), define water productivity as **WP = Y/I**, where Y is crop yield (kg/ha) and **I is irrigation water applied (m³/ha)**. The authors describe WSC-flume inlet flow for surface plots and timed nozzle/dripper discharge for pressurized systems. This identifies a *field irrigation-input productivity* contrast, not independently measured crop evapotranspiration, net groundwater depletion, basin-level water withdrawals, or long-term hydrologic conservation. In F7, the source reports **292.7 mm greater applied irrigation depth** in the DSS section and **lower irrigation-input WP** than the control. It is therefore inaccurate to use this case as evidence of universal irrigation-water saving or a positive F7 productivity response. The source abstract's **1.5% improvement for “surface irrigation systems”** is a group-level author-reported summary whose aggregation weights and case-level consistency cannot be reconstructed from the inspected passage; it must **not** override the adverse F7 site-level direction or be presented as an improvement on *every* surface-irrigated farm. [Original publisher, Methods Eq. (3), Table 4, Results Table 5 and Abstract](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-26567-z). This resolves the **metric-definition and scope-of-inference error**, not the source's contradictory exact F7 WP decrement (−1.3/−1.4/−0.1 kg/m³); the latter remains **BLOCKED** pending original unrounded data/corrigendum.

**C131 F7 yield/depth cross-check (30 October 2026; original published values, not unrounded records).** Original [Amini et al. (2025), Table 4](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-26567-z.pdf) prints F7 seasonal gross irrigation depths of **1,057.6 mm** (DSS) and **764.9 mm** (control), and Table 5 prints apple yields of **48.9** and **45.6 t/ha**. Because 1 t/ha = 1,000 kg/ha and 1 mm over a hectare = 10 m³/ha, WP (kg/m³) = **100 × yield (t/ha) / irrigation depth (mm)**. On the *displayed published values*, WP is **4.6237** (DSS) versus **5.9616** (control), giving **−1.3379 kg/m³** (about **−1.3** when rounded after division). By contrast, subtracting the *separately rounded* Table 4 WP entries gives **4.6 − 6.0 = −1.4 kg/m³**. Thus the source's two Results values (**−1.3** and **−1.4**) are **arithmetically compatible with two rounding pathways** and should no longer be classified as a demonstrated substantive disagreement. This does **not** authenticate underlying unrounded farm measurements. Relative WP change from displayed yield/depth entries is approximately **−22.44%**, versus **−23.33%** from separately rounded Table 4 WP entries. Neither reconciles Table 5's **−3.22** in its percent-labelled WP column or the Conclusion's **−0.1 kg/m³**; those remain **BLOCKED_SOURCE_NUMERIC**. F7 irrigation increased by **292.7 mm**, equivalent to **38.27%** of control; published yield entries differ by **3.3 t/ha**, whereas Results prose reports **3.4 t/ha**, another small rounded-data/source-prose ambiguity. No F7-specific significance, event-level adherence, causal generalization or corrected author-reported percentage is inferred. [Publisher PDF, Tables 4–5, Results and Conclusion](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-26567-z.pdf).

The publisher text reports a paired t-test at p < 0.05, but the inspected passage does not supply an F7-specific test statistic, paired observation count, degrees of freedom or variance for the 1.3/1.4/0.1 discrepancy. The paper's eight purposively selected monitored farms are not 80 randomized treatment replicates. Therefore a reported pooled significance statement cannot validate any particular F7 water-productivity delta, resolve a textual inconsistency or establish a population causal effect. This review reports the published significance claim only with its design and verification limitations; it does not recompute a p-value. [Amini et al. 2025, Results and Methods](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-26567-z).

### C132: conditional F7 rounding-bound sensitivity (30 October 2026)

The previously transcribed original Amini Tables 4–5 display F7 treatment/control yields of **48.9/45.6 t ha−1** and applied irrigation depths of **1057.6/764.9 mm**. Under the source's irrigation-input WP definition, **WP (kg m−3) = 100 × yield (t ha−1) / applied depth (mm)**. Direct evaluation gives **4.62368/5.96156 kg m−3**, or **−1.33789 kg m−3** for treatment minus control. This reconciles the Results' **−1.3 kg m−3** after rounding to one decimal; separately subtracting Table 4's rounded WP cells **4.6 − 6.0 = −1.4 kg m−3** is a different order of rounding.

A conditional interval audit assumes the *displayed yield and depth* were independently rounded to the nearest **0.1 t ha−1** and **0.1 mm**, respectively, with no other transcription or source error. Propagating ±0.05 around each displayed input yields an F7 difference between **−1.34976 and −1.32601 kg m−3**. Every value in this narrow *rounding-sensitivity interval* rounds to −1.3 at one decimal; it is **not a confidence interval**, independent raw-data verification, or a new estimate of treatment effect. It does not resolve Table 5's **−3.22** in a percent-labelled column or the Conclusion's **−0.1 kg m−3**; those remain quarantined pending source-author clarification, authenticated erratum, or explicit qualified reviewer limitation decision.

### 3.2 Cvejić et al. (2020): implementation, feedback and modeled savings

The [original publisher article](https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4395/10/9/1238) describes 35 farms expressing interest in 2016 (207.15 ha), followed by 38.5 ha included after agreements. Interested farms, interested area and signed area are distinct denominators; 38.5/207.15=18.59% is an *area ratio*, not a farmer-retention rate. The abstract's 35 implementation farms does not establish the complete signed-farm roster. A 2019 passage reports 46 farmers receiving advice by e-mail, incompatible with treating every year and question as a single 35-person frame. Without dated rosters the 2019 and 2020 feedback and the 2020 reported 83% IDSS use or 71% regular soil-moisture checks cannot be assigned reconstructed item counts. Incomplete irrigation diaries further limit behavioral inference.

The study's reported 25% irrigation-volume, 24% energy and 24% CO₂ savings are **conditional modeled scenarios**, not realized conservation. Table A10's numerical annual energy savings are in **kWh** despite a conflicting volume-unit caption. Three 2017 modeled rows reconcile against each farm's modeled FC electricity requirement: ID 1: 889.91−700.43=189.48 kWh (21.29%); ID 4: 4340.36−2194.25=2146.11 kWh (49.45%); ID 6: 15051.41−11754.98=3296.43 kWh (21.90%). These checks validate arithmetic and unit interpretation, not independent pump-meter observations. Shorter but more frequent irrigations do not identify total water volume without discharge, event count and duration. [Original publisher tables](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy10091238).

**Disposition:** modeled row arithmetic is usable with explicit labels; 2019 e-mail-user frame, signed-farm identities, item-specific 2020 response denominators and realized savings remain BLOCKED.

### 3.3 Jamal et al. (2023): observed field inputs and virtual human decisions

This work integrates field observations, optimization and a virtual farmer scheduling exercise. Its simulated decisions and modeled outcomes must not be counted as additional physical irrigation events. Workshop participants, group-level choices and field inputs have different denominators and cannot be pooled as independent farm trials. The study is valuable for examining how humans react to schedules under stated assumptions, not for independently verifying realized water conservation from virtual decisions. [Original publication](https://doi.org/10.1029/2023WR035810).

### 3.4 Abi Saab et al. (2019): replicated research-station irrigation comparison

At the Tal Amara experimental station, nine plots represented three treatments with three plot replicates. Authors report Bluleaf-guided versus customary gross irrigation depths of **270 versus 360 mm** in one season and **331 versus 450 mm** in another, alongside field measurement and crop outcomes. The rounded seasonal contrasts correspond to **25.0%** and **26.4%** less irrigation relative to customary plots; the article's separately reported pooled **25.7%** should not be reconstructed from rounded values without original plot-level records. These are experimental-plot agronomic outcomes, not nine independent commercial farms or proof of voluntary grower adoption. [Original publication](https://doi.org/10.3390/w11020252).

### 3.5 Car et al. (2012): SMS engagement and conflicting respondent frames

The [University of New England original institutional record](https://researchers.une.edu.au/en/publications/using-a-mobile-phone-short-messaging-service-sms-for-irrigation-s-5/) reports 72 irrigators: 45 returned inputs all season, 13 for about half a season and 14 never returned inputs (45+13+14=72). This is an engagement-duration classification, **not** independently measured implementation of daily irrigation advice.

The [author-uploaded typeset original, §3.4 and Tables 4–6](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257018309_Using_a_mobile_phone_Short_Messaging_Service_SMS_for_irrigation_scheduling_in_Australia_-_Farmers%27_participation_and_utility_evaluation) prints Table 5 counts 26, 7, 3, 6, 15 and 17 (sum **74** despite total **72**); printed percentages sum **103.2%**. The names of groups 4 and 5 are reversed between numbered descriptions and Table 5. The prose's stopped-group 7/12 conflicts with the printed stopped count 15; the abstract's 14 nonreporters conflicts with Table 5's 17. No original roster or authenticated erratum has been obtained. No prevalence is reconstructed from Table 5.

Table 6 instead prints five perceived-behavior categories 11, 10, 9, 8 and 3 (sum **41**) while prose says **40** respondents. Its printed percentages and cumulative 46%, 73% and 80% can be reproduced from 41 *category assignments* (19/41, 30/41, 33/41), but not authenticated as 41 distinct people or verified behavior changes. ### 3.6 Elia et al. (2022): commercial tomato farms and daily smartphone advice

The [2022 ISHS publisher abstract](https://www.actahort.org/books/1335/1335_81.htm) reports **six commercial processing-tomato farms** in Capitanata (Foggia, Italy), each divided into **two management plots**: one following daily GesCoN fertigation advice and one following that farm's empirical grower practice. Weather input was automated and advice communicated through **daily smartphone notifications**. The publisher reports average **24% lower seasonal irrigation water volume** and **7% lower N fertilizer application** in the DSS-managed comparison, without a detected total or marketable yield difference. These are source-reported farm/plot-season contrasts, **not six randomized independent farm trials plus six further independent controls**, and the abstract does not document random allocation, calibrated independent flow metering, plot-level uncertainty, actual receipt logs, event-level deviations or later voluntary use. The six farms are the primary clustering context; twelve management plots must not be naively treated as twelve independent farms or as recommendation-event observations.

**Original GesCoN methods crosswalk (source crosswalk, not a reconstruction of the 2022 trial protocol).** [Elia and Conversa (2015), Part I, “Operational management of fertigation”](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2015.00319/full), describes software that produces per-event water and N advice, asks the operator to **confirm a scheduled fertigation using a checkbox**, and requires the operator to enter corrected amounts if applied water or N differs. Such software entries would be **operator-entered implementation records**, not independently metered actions by virtue of being stored in a DSS. The accessible 2022 abstract does **not** establish whether its commercial-farm implementation used this exact interface, whether operators completed the fields, or whether independent action verification was available; those links remain UNKNOWN. Therefore a theoretical ability to record a completed event must not be reported as verified 2022 receipt-to-action fidelity.

**Model-validation versus field-implementation distinction (new the preceding source audit).** [Conversa et al. (2015), Part II](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2015.00495/full), reports **five calibration trial datasets** and **thirteen validation datasets** for model predictions of tomato growth and N uptake; these are not eighteen commercial farms in the 2018 GesCoN evaluation, nor independent evidence that 2018 growers followed smartphone advice. The published 2015 validation statistics address predictive model fit, not the 2022 farm-paired behavioral estimand. **the preceding source audit correction 1 — null-hypothesis results do not establish yield equivalence.** The [ISHS original publisher abstract](https://www.actahort.org/books/1335/1335_81.htm) states that no significant differences in total/marketable yield or selected fruit-quality traits were detected between management plots. This is a *failure to detect a difference*, not a prespecified equivalence/non-inferiority test: the accessible abstract provides neither a yield-equivalence margin nor confidence intervals and farm-cluster power analysis. The reported 24% irrigation-volume and 7% N reductions are averages for the six-farm paired context; they are not guaranteed farm-specific reductions, independently replicated effects, or proof that equivalent yield is established. The full text, farm-pair outcome table, uncertainty and allocation are necessary before any yield-preservation or causal non-inferiority claim.

The complete 2022 trial Methods, assignment, raw water/N records and field-action provenance remain **BLOCKED** before a stronger causal or adherence inference. [2022 publisher abstract](https://doi.org/10.17660/ActaHortic.2022.1335.81).

### 3.7 Baroni et al. (2019): method comparison is not a farmer-adherence denominator

The [original Wiley publisher abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ird.2337), published 4 April 2019, reports **two distinct data-generating activities**: direct end-user interviews and a comparative assessment of **six irrigation-scheduling methods**. The authors found broad agreement among **five of the six methods on estimated seasonal irrigation amounts**, but greater disagreement in the **timing of the irrigation season and number of irrigation events**. Positive interview feedback coexisted with a qualitative finding that recommendations were rarely implemented; the abstract says seasonal irrigation requirements were considered for actual water abstraction. Neither the method count (six) nor the five-method agreement numerator identifies a sample of farmers, observed irrigation events or measured voluntary uptake. Consequently, the numerical fraction **5/6 is not an adoption rate**, and the word *rarely* supplies no extractable adherence percentage. Agreement on a seasonal sum does not establish event-by-event agreement, actual application, water conservation or causal impact.

**Evidence-access limit:** the Wiley page lists a supporting file, `ird2337-sup-0001-Data_S1.docx`, but its download returned HTTP 403 on 30 October 2026. This file was **not inspected**, so no participant counts, interview coding or hidden field measurements are imputed from it. The accessible publisher abstract supports the above narrow comparison; the complete methods and supplement remain required before any numerical farmer-implementation estimate. [Publisher record and DOI](https://doi.org/10.1002/ird.2337).

### 3.8 Darouich et al. (2024): farmer schedules compared with optimized alternatives

The original post-evaluation addresses 20 center-pivot maize plots in Portugal and directly examines farmer schedules relative to optimized recommendations. Weekly applied irrigation information was collected via technician surveys, with temporary assumptions of compliance when entries were delayed. Modeled evapotranspiration and yield for alternative schedules must not be presented as directly measured causal gains. The publisher's abstract names **2019 and 2020**, while Methods §2.3 and the Results discuss **2019 and 2022**; this is a source-internal year conflict, not two independently verified comparisons. ## 5. Discussion: what the evidence does and does not connect

The cases separate three propositions frequently bundled into a single success label. Experimental and commercial plot studies can support **within-study agronomic contrasts**, although assignment, measurement and uncertainty determine causal strength. Messaging and survey studies can support **participation or reported-use claims**, without proving advice-specific irrigation actions. Simulation and optimization can support **scenario potential**, without establishing realized savings. Pooling the Lebanese experimental contrast, Italian commercial-farm contrast and Slovenian modeled 25% scenario as a common water-saving effect would violate their differing designs and denominators.

A prospective reporting template could record six links: (1) recommendation identifier, field and timestamp; (2) delivery and evidence of receipt; (3) independently documented action, including departures from advice; (4) calibrated physical water and crop measurements; (5) comparator, assignment and uncertainty; and (6) later voluntary use or transfer of learned heuristics. The original G/D/R/A shorthand should not be treated as a validated scale, and no retrospective score is computed here. Existing Lorite advisory-comparison, telemetry, Baroni implementation and Ara adoption research already cover major parts of this terrain. The potential benefit of a linked audit trail is **claim transparency**; its novelty and inter-rater reliability remain untested.

Two threats to synthesis deserve special attention. First, repeated plots, farms, respondents, advice events and simulated seasons are not interchangeable independent replicates. Second, multiple publications from a common scheme may share sites or personnel: the Genil–Cabra reports from 2008–2013 show geographic and investigator overlap, although duplicate plot observations have not been proven. Neither source should be counted as an independent replication without inspecting its sampling frame and observation dates.

## 5.1. Proposed, falsifiable source-to-claim recording protocol (not validated)

A prospective record should include: a unique recommendation ID, timestamp and plot/crop; advice specificity/type and any explicit action target; numerical advice (depth, duration or date) and unit; issue, delivery and actual-receipt evidence as separate fields; independently measured action and its timestamp; calibrated water and crop measurements; weather and allocation context; assignment, comparator and farm/season clustering; and later voluntary reuse. For event-level adherence, an observation is an **eligible actionable recommendation-event**, not a farmer, farm or informational message; informational exposure is tracked separately. The accessible source material inspected for this review has not established a complete prospective event ledger; this does not exclude the possibility of uninspected original records.

**Issued-advice estimand and structural nonreceipt rule.** Restricting the denominator to recommendations with *verifiable action linkage* would select on successful follow-up and can inflate implementation. Define the primary population as all prospectively eligible, deduplicated **actionable prescriptive recommendations** (or conditional recommendations with an observed trigger and defined response) **issued** in a prespecified farm/time window (\(N\)); record separate stage counts for successful delivery, verified receipt and measured action. These stage counts need not share the same observational denominator and cannot be used interchangeably. A delivery- or receipt-conditional proportion, if presented, is a secondary estimand with its own explicit population and missingness assumptions.

Classify each eligible issued recommendation exactly once, with a source-backed audit trail: **I** = verified implementation (independently verified receipt followed by time-linked measured action within a prespecified tolerance); **D** = verified departure after verified receipt, comprising either (a) a time-linked measured action outside the prespecified target/window/tolerance or (b) independently established **absence of the prescribed action throughout an adequately observed action window**. A missing sensor record or absent diary is **U**, never evidence of a verified zero action; **B** = *verified nonreceipt* or definitive failed delivery, with no alternate receipt channel and an auditable observation window, so the strict **received-and-implemented** event cannot have occurred; **U** = unresolved or unadjudicable, including unknown receipt, missing action measurement, ambiguous event linkage or unspecified tolerance. A delivery failure alone is not B if another delivery channel may have reached the farmer; classify as U unless nonreceipt is actually established. An irrigation action resembling the advice but occurring without verified receipt does **not** satisfy I. Thus **\(N=I+D+B+U\)**, with nonnegative disjoint counts. Preserve timestamps, channels, late receipt and measurement failures as auxiliary flags without double-counting events.

For **\(N>0\)**, the fraction of issued recommendations satisfying the strict receipt-and-implementation definition is bounded by **\([I/N,(I+U)/N]\)**. The lower endpoint counts only verified implementations; the upper assigns all *unresolved* events, but **not structurally verified nonreceipt B**, to potential implementation. Both are arithmetic identification bounds, **not confidence intervals, empirical adherence estimates or causal effects**. If \(N=0\), no fraction is defined. The **receipt-and-action-resolved conditional fraction** **\(I/(I+D)\)** is reportable only if \(I+D>0\), explicitly conditional on verified receipt and adequately adjudicated action; verified nonreceipt **B** is already resolved but intentionally excluded from this secondary receipt-conditional denominator. This is **not** the fraction of all fully adjudicated issued events; it must not be presented as the issued-population fraction. If no verifiable nonreceipt category can be established, **\(B=0\)** and the upper bound remains conservative, provided the structural-nonreceipt classification is not assumed without evidence. In particular, a self-report of receipt, a server-side delivery flag and a time-linked physical action represent distinct evidentiary links.

**Interpretation limit.** The bound applies to the explicitly operationalized *verified receipt-and-implementation* process, not to the unobservable counterfactual question of whether a farmer would have irrigated similarly without seeing the advice. If the intended outcome is instead *physical agreement with the recommendation regardless of receipt*, the B category is not a structural zero and this tighter upper bound is invalid; the estimand and its bounds must be redefined. Prespecify farm/season clustering, event windows, tolerance, calibration and adjudication rules before field collection. No reviewed paper has been demonstrated, **from the source material inspected here**, to supply every link of this prospective event ledger; this does not assert that uninspected original records do not exist.

Any future causal water-saving claim requires a separately specified counterfactual estimand, assignment mechanism, comparator and uncertainty, with farm/season clustering or repeated-measures dependence addressed. Event-level process proportions, even with complete data, cannot by themselves establish attributable water savings or sustained voluntary use. Independent blinded coding, inter-rater reliability, prespecified sensitivity analyses and prospective validation remain necessary before the template or its classifications could be described as validated. **No numeric adherence, bounds or causal effect is calculated in this review because the required event-level data do not exist here.**

For each quantitative claim, archive its original table/figure locator, source version, observation unit, numerator, denominator, comparator, measurement provenance, period and unresolved contradiction. Classify it as *source-reported*, *arithmetically reconstructed*, *modeled*, or *not independently verified*. Do not substitute a reconstructed denominator (for example Cvejić's plausible 35) for a source-authenticated item-response ledger, or treat a conflicting Car transcription as the original publisher table. ## 5.3. Advice specificity and identifiability gate (conceptual, not validated)

A recommendation-delivery-action (G/D/R/A) audit is only interpretable if the **action requested by the message is operationally defined before outcomes are inspected**. Record `advice_type` as: **prescriptive** (explicit target volume/depth/duration and action window), **conditional** (a specified rule or trigger with observable antecedent), or **informational/non-prescriptive** (water balance, indicative run time or contextual guidance without a required irrigation event). This is a proposed editorial taxonomy, **not a validated instrument or a retrospective reclassification of all eight studies**.

For a prescriptive message, adherence may be adjudicated only when a prespecified action target, time window, tolerance, independently documented applied action, and relevant exceptions are available. For a conditional message, verify both the triggering condition and the specified response. For an informational message, **binary adherence is undefined** absent an agreed action rule; instead report verified delivery, receipt, consideration, subsequent decision and measured field outcome as separate constructs. A missing prescriptive target must **not** be coded as farmer noncompliance or as a zero in an adherence denominator.

This matters for Car et al. (2012): although its SMS communicated dripper run-time guidance, the authors explicitly state that the service did not stipulate when or how much to irrigate, and respondents combined the guidance with other information. Their 25% multi-day accumulation and 79% multi-source decision reports lack authenticated item-specific denominators. Neither percentage, the Table 5 'Close' label, nor the 53/72 consideration measure can be transformed into a verified event-specific compliance estimate. The protocol's **recommendation-event denominator is restricted to eligible, operationally prescriptive events**; non-prescriptive advice belongs to a separately reported informational-exposure denominator. This avoids penalizing farmers for discretion expressly allowed by the advisory design.

**Reproducible classification check:** If a message has no explicit action target/window and no trigger-response rule, classify its event-level adherence as `NOT_DEFINED`, not `0` or `1`. If the original wording is unavailable, use `UNKNOWN_ADVICE_TYPE`, not `NONCOMPLIANT`. These are logic safeguards, not observed trial results.

## 5.4. Seasonal recommendation ratios are not event-level fidelity

The [Lorite et al. (2012) indexed original abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/waterr/v26y2012i8p2397-2419.html) reports crop-specific **seasonal** actual-to-advisory irrigation ratios around 0.23–0.94. Such ratios do not measure the proportion of advice events implemented and cannot identify the reasons for deviations. The study's IWP and IWB indicators are monetary EUR/m³ measures, not physical kg/m³ yield or compliance rates; full-text economic inputs remain to be authenticated.

[Baroni et al. (2019)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ird.2337) compare **six scheduling methods**, not six farmers or six independently observed actions. Broad agreement on seasonal amounts does not establish agreement on event timing or actual grower implementation. The publisher lists Data S1, but its contents have not been inspected. Related 2003 modeled-optimum, 2010 satellite/FAO indicator and [2013 telemetry](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.compag.2013.08.007) studies in Genil–Cabra concern different years and estimands, not three replications of event-level adherence.

**Non-pooling rule:** keep seasonal water-quantity ratios, independently linked recommendation-event actions, and physical outcome contrasts in separate columns. None identifies the others without corresponding original records.

## 6. Limitations

**Scope restriction:** Car Table 3's 223/238 correct system-days cannot be multiplied by the 72 trial participants or compared directly with the institutional abstract's 200-day description without a dated service-window and individual-delivery roster. Table 1's explicitly multiple-response 209 tool mentions do not supply a legitimate correction to the contradictory Table 5 partition. Both claims rely on the author-uploaded typeset article, not a separately authenticated Elsevier version-of-record or erratum.


This purposive eight-case corpus cannot estimate the prevalence of DSS effectiveness or adoption. Full 2022 commercial-farm methods for Elia, full Lorite and Baroni methods, and the original publisher image of Car's disputed table remain unavailable to this version. The independently accessible 2015 GesCoN software description and model-validation papers clarify possible operator-recording mechanisms and model fit but **do not** authenticate the 2022 trial's implementation records. Car Table 5's group-4/group-5 prose-versus-table reversal and nonconserving counts prevent water-balance category prevalence extraction; the preceding source audit's minimum-edit analysis excludes a one-row correction under a shared-roster assumption; the preceding source audit's 58/72 ever-reporter fraction cannot validate the abstract's subjective 80% utility assertion or prove receipt-to-action compliance, and the preceding source audit Table 6 sensitivity bounds cannot identify a missing or duplicated respondent; Table 6 influence percentages are quarantined in the source appendix because its 41-count table conflicts with the 40-person prose; neither semantic nor arithmetic checks are independent source validation; Amini F7 source statements and Cvejić item-response denominators are unresolved despite exclusion of 46 as the 2020 two-person/6% denominator. Source access, coding and the conceptual reporting template have not undergone independent human dual-review, inter-rater testing or prospective field validation. There is no new dataset, no registered protocol, no pooled effect, no independently observed G/D/R/A sequence and no claim of causal water savings produced by this manuscript. These limitations must remain visible in any later journal-facing version.

## 7. Conclusion

A recommendation delivered, a farmer engaged, an irrigation event implemented and a measured water outcome are distinct evidentiary claims. The eight illustrative cases show why their observational units and source provenance should remain explicit. The contribution of this working review is a consolidated critical comparison and a **proposed, unvalidated source-to-claim reporting discipline**, not a novel empirical effect or a first-in-field adoption framework. Original-source reconciliation, substantive human-author scientific judgment and journal-specific compliance are prerequisites for a final manuscript.
