# From Irrigation Recommendations to Field Outcomes: An Evidence-Provenance Critical Narrative Review

**Internal identifier:** MR-2026-001 · **Clean author-review manuscript C87 (2026-10-29)** · **Status: READY_FOR_REVIEW, not submitted or author-approved.**

**Article type:** Critical narrative review of eight purposively selected studies; not a systematic review, scoping review, meta-analysis or validated empirical protocol. The intended sole human author must make a substantive scholarly contribution and approve the exact text, disclosures and journal selection before submission. All cited DOIs identify *prior published works*, not this manuscript.

## Abstract

Irrigation decision-support systems (DSS) can generate agronomically plausible advice without necessarily changing farmers' actions or producing independently attributable water savings. This critical narrative review examines eight purposively selected published examples spanning farm-scale comparisons, research-station plots, advisory messaging, farmer-reported use, and virtual decision exercises. We distinguish physical measurements from reported perceptions, observed implementation, and model-based counterfactuals, while retaining the farm, plot, person, irrigation event and season as non-interchangeable units of analysis. The reviewed reports include measured irrigation contrasts, daily digital advice, participation records and simulated benefits; none of these evidence types alone establishes sustained voluntary adoption or causal conservation. Several numerical claims require particular caution: the accessible Car et al. (2012) Table 5 transcription conflicts with its own total and the institutional abstract; Amini et al. (2025) report inconsistent F7 water-productivity differences; and the 2020 Cvejić survey denominator cannot be established merely from its 35 implementation farms. Earlier studies already address adoption, advice-versus-applied irrigation, telemetry and grower comparisons. Accordingly, the possible incremental contribution of this review is **transparent source-to-claim adjudication**, not priority for studying adoption or a newly validated compliance measure. We propose an explicitly unvalidated reporting template linking recommendation, delivery, independently documented action, field outcome, comparator and later voluntary use. Missing full methods, heterogeneous designs and author verification preclude a pooled effect estimate or general prevalence claim.

**Keywords:** irrigation decision support; evidence provenance; farmer uptake; implementation fidelity; water productivity; narrative review.

## 1. Introduction

Technical validity, behavioral implementation and hydrologic outcomes are distinct propositions. A model may accurately predict crop water demand; an advisory service may reach a phone; a participant may report that advice is useful. None of these observations establishes that a specified irrigation recommendation was implemented, that applied volume was independently measured, or that an observed difference is attributable to the advice rather than weather, water allocation, crop choice or other changes. A rigorous synthesis must specify the observation and its denominator before translating an outcome into a claim.

The literature already addresses these distinctions. Ara et al. (2021) review DSS adoption, economics and the possibility that growers retain useful decision heuristics even after ceasing to use software. Lorite et al. (2012) compare actual irrigation with advisory recommendations, while Lorite et al. (2013) report field telemetry in the same named irrigation district. Baroni et al. (2019) document limited implementation despite favorable system assessments. Elia et al. (2022) report daily smartphone advice and commercial-farm comparisons. Berthold et al. (2021) study barriers to irrigation-scheduling-tool adoption with mailed questionnaires; their 77 usable responses and item-specific response denominators cannot be recast as objective advice-event adherence. These precedents rule out any assertion that the present manuscript first discovered the adoption gap, field observation, mobile delivery or farmer-versus-recommendation comparison.

The narrow editorial question is instead: **what evidence supports each specific claim about a recommendation, an action and a water outcome, and where does the inferential chain break?** The aim is to illustrate how source-level provenance and explicit missingness can improve the interpretation of heterogeneous studies. Whether this approach adds enough scholarly value beyond existing reviews remains for the intended human author and an independent journal review to judge.

## 2. Methods: scope, selection and coding

This is a **non-systematic critical narrative review**. Eight studies were selected purposively from documented bibliographic discovery and subsequent targeted source checking because they illustrate different combinations of field measurement, farmer reporting, advisory delivery and modeled scenarios. The set is neither an exhaustive sampling frame nor a denominator for estimating global DSS adoption or effectiveness. Earlier repository retrieval counts (672 bibliographic entries and 604 machine-matched candidate citations) are discovery-process records, **not PRISMA screening counts or a completed systematic search**. No independent dual-human screening, protocol registration or meta-analysis is asserted.

**Transparent post hoc case-selection rationale (C86; not a prospective search protocol).** The eight cases are an editorially purposive set selected to expose distinct inference breaks, not a sample with known inclusion probabilities. Their roles in the argument are specified below so a reader can challenge their selection; this table does **not** establish that these were prespecified eligibility rules or that all eligible literature was found.

Lorite's published advisory-versus-applied seasonal comparison and telemetry work, and Ara's adoption review, are discussed as **contextual prior art**, not silently counted among the eight. This editorial distinction is not evidence of their lesser scientific quality. The manuscript offers no PRISMA flow, exhaustive exclusion list, dual-screening record, search cutoff reproducibility or pooled effect estimate; these absences constrain generalization and must be disclosed in any journal submission.

For each example, we distinguish **M**: directly reported physical field measurement; **O**: independently documented implementation of a particular recommendation; **S**: self-report, survey or participant appraisal; **P**: prediction, optimization or simulated counterfactual; and **U**: an unresolved or insufficiently accessible source component. These are descriptive provenance labels, **not a validated ordinal quality score**. A study can contain several classes; a measured input used to validate a model does not turn the model's hypothetical output into a realized effect.

The unit of inference is recorded as person, farm, plot, irrigation event, crop-season or simulated decision. Where original full methods are unavailable, absence of evidence in the inspected abstract must not be presented as proof that the original investigators never collected that evidence. Numerical contradictions are flagged rather than repaired through speculative arithmetic. The intended human author has **not yet finalized study selection, source interpretation, conflict-of-interest disclosures or the final manuscript**.

## 3. Eight illustrative cases

### 3.1 Amini et al. (2025): within-farm comparison and unresolved F7 reporting

The original open-access article describes eight monitored farms/orchards selected from 80 candidate farms in four villages in the Lake Urmia basin, with customary and DSS-guided sections. Water delivery and yield were assessed at the section level and farmers participated in workshops. This is a real on-farm agronomic comparison, not 80 independently randomized treatment replicates. The source's F7 farm is displayed in **Figure 12**, not Figure 7 (whose caption depicts F3). Table 4 displays F7 water productivity rounded to one decimal as **4.6 versus 6.0 kg/m³**; subtracting the displayed values gives **−1.4 kg/m³**. However, under conventional nearest-tenth rounding the underlying difference could lie approximately between **−1.50 and −1.30 kg/m³**, so the Results' **−1.3 kg/m³** may be compatible with unrounded measurements and is **not, by itself, a demonstrated error**. The conclusion's **−0.1 kg/m³** lies outside that rounding interval and remains unexplained absent an alternative definition, correction or underlying observations. No single exact F7 effect is inferred from rounded values. The treatment received **292.7 mm more** irrigation for F7 (**1057.6−764.9 mm** in Table 4); this is a water-depth contrast, not a direct measure of adherence. These discrepancies prevent a confidently reconstructed F7 effect and any pooled estimate from the rounded publication tables. The authors' participatory observations do not demonstrate later unsupported voluntary use. [Original publication](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-26567-z); [C55 source audit](MR-2026-001_C55_AMINI_F7_FIGURE_CROSSWALK_AND_CLUSTER_INFERENCE_2026-10-27.md).

### 3.2 Cvejić et al. (2020): implementation farms, telephone feedback and modeled savings

The open-access publisher abstract describes an IDSS implemented on **35 farms** in 2016–2020, while Methods §2.1 more precisely says **35 farms expressed interest** at the 2016 public call (covering 207.15 ha) and later reports **38.5 ha included after participation agreements**; these are not automatically identical enrollment, signed-area and response frames. The Results distinguish **2019 first-experience feedback from 66% of 'our farmers'** and **2020 telephone feedback described as coming from all farmers**. These year-specific frames cannot be combined without a dated respondent ledger. The Methods describe interviews, workshops and field inventories and the 2020 three-weekly phone follow-ups; **91% reportedly did not regularly complete irrigation diaries**, making missingness central to outcome interpretation. The Results say *all farmers* gave feedback in 2020, and report **83%** using the IDSS, **71%** checking soil moisture regularly and **6% (two farmers)** dissatisfied. Although some reported percentages are arithmetically compatible with a 35-person denominator, **the respondent count for each question has not been authenticated**. Accordingly, no reconstructed item counts (such as 29/35 or 25/35) are used as observed data or evidence that every question shared a common response frame. The reported **6% (two farmers)** is retained only as the publisher's own statement. **Source-frame gate (C81):** the publisher's **2019** Results literally state that **46 farmers use irrigation advice by e-mail**, whereas the abstract gives 35 farms and Methods identifies 35 interested farms in 2016. This literal count belongs to the **2019** passage and must not be reassigned to the separate **2020** telephone feedback. This apparent 46-versus-35 discrepancy cannot be explained by silently inventing an additional sample, converting 46 into a percentage, or assigning it to the 2020 questionnaire. It may be a source typo, a different respondent frame, or a transcription issue; none is verified. Exclude the 46-farmer count from evidence extraction until the original authors or an authenticated correction establish its meaning. The reported **25% irrigation-volume saving is a conditional model scenario**, not independently observed realized conservation. Institutional accounts of subsequent SPON operation further show that sustained advisory infrastructure predates this review but do not identify event-level farmer adherence. [Original publication](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy10091238); [C63 denominator gate](MR-2026-001_C63_CVEJIC_35_FARM_ABSTRACT_VS_2020_SURVEY_DENOMINATOR_2026-10-28.md).

### 3.3 Jamal et al. (2023): observed field inputs and virtual human decisions

This work integrates field observations, optimization and a virtual farmer scheduling exercise. Its simulated decisions and modeled outcomes must not be counted as additional physical irrigation events. Workshop participants, group-level choices and field inputs have different denominators and cannot be pooled as independent farm trials. The study is valuable for examining how humans react to schedules under stated assumptions, not for independently verifying realized water conservation from virtual decisions. [Original publication](https://doi.org/10.1029/2023WR035810).

### 3.4 Abi Saab et al. (2019): replicated research-station irrigation comparison

At the Tal Amara experimental station, nine plots represented three treatments with three plot replicates. Authors report Bluleaf-guided versus customary gross irrigation depths of **270 versus 360 mm** in one season and **331 versus 450 mm** in another, alongside field measurement and crop outcomes. The rounded seasonal contrasts correspond to **25.0%** and **26.4%** less irrigation relative to customary plots; the article's separately reported pooled **25.7%** should not be reconstructed from rounded values without original plot-level records. These are experimental-plot agronomic outcomes, not nine independent commercial farms or proof of voluntary grower adoption. [Original publication](https://doi.org/10.3390/w11020252).

### 3.5 Car et al. (2012): separate participation and behavioral denominators

The [University of New England institutional record](https://rune.une.edu.au/entities/publication/68f9d1ef-6e62-415a-b831-0beb96df9853) reports 72 irrigators: 45 returned data throughout, 13 for half a season and 14 never returned data (45+13+14=72). This is an engagement-duration classification, **not independently observed event-level compliance**. The [author-uploaded printed article](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257018309_Using_a_mobile_phone_Short_Messaging_Service_SMS_for_irrigation_scheduling_in_Australia_-_Farmers%27_participation_and_utility_evaluation), pp. 139–141, reports Table 5 counts 26, 7, 3, 6, 15, 17 (sum **74**, against printed total 72), with printed percentages totaling **103.2%**. The Table 5 **17** in the *Never sent* row additionally conflicts with the [UNE institutional abstract](https://researchers.une.edu.au/en/publications/using-a-mobile-phone-short-messaging-service-sms-for-irrigation-s-5/)'s **14** never-reporters; no one-to-one crosswalk of the two frames has been authenticated. It labels Group 4 *Much less* and Group 5 *Stopped*, while preceding numbered prose defines Group 4 *Stopped sending* and Group 5 *Less throughout*. The prose's 68% for Groups 1–3 conflicts with 36/72=50%; its stopping subgroup 7/12 is not reconciled with either Table 5 count. These **Table 5** inconsistencies remain unresolved.

**Main-text admissibility decision:** The author's Table 5 water-balance categories do not conserve the stated 72-person total, and Table 6's five behavior categories total 41 although adjacent prose reports 40 respondents. The reported cumulative **46%/73%/80% perceived-influence figures** can be reconstructed arithmetically and mapped semantically *within the same author-uploaded source* (the separately retained C86 source-adjudication appendix), but these are **not independent confirmations** of the questionnaire denominator, coding or participant behavior. **Exclude these disputed percentages from the primary eight-case evidence matrix and any cross-study numerical synthesis**; preserve the calculations only in the explicitly quarantined source-audit appendix for possible author/publisher adjudication. The discrepancies are neither silently corrected nor described as demonstrated noncompliance. 

**Distinct, positively extractable outcome:** the same printed full text (p. 139, §3.4) reports **53 of 72 irrigators (73.61%, rounded in source to 74%)** saying that they actively *considered* the DSS in scheduling decisions. This is a participant-reported **decision-input** outcome, not independently verified implementation of a particular recommendation. It can be described alongside the UNE record's **45/72 (62.5%)** full-season message-return outcome, but their overlap is not known; neither fraction is an event-level compliance rate. The discussion also reports **69% of the original 72 wished to use the system the following season**, and **83% among full-season users**; these are reported *intentions* rather than observed subsequent-season adoption, and the source does not supply a verified cross-tabulation. Do not convert 69% or 83% into invented exact counts.

Neither the coherent Table 6 arithmetic nor the 45/72 institutional engagement summary establishes recommendation-event adherence, a causal reduction in water use, or a generalizable population prevalence. The institutional abstract's **80% perceived usefulness** is a distinct appraisal claim whose numerator cannot be reconstructed from the accompanying counts: **45** full-season reporters plus **six** of the **13** partial-season reporters who regretted stopping yields **51/72 = 70.83%**, not 80%; adding **all 13** partial reporters yields **58/72 = 80.56%**, but the abstract does not establish that all 13 rated the system useful. These are alternative arithmetic illustrations, **not corrected source data**. The original item-specific usefulness numerator and coding remain unknown. Retain 80% only as an author-reported appraisal, never as independently verified adherence, an observed action or a recalculated prevalence. [UNE institutional abstract](https://researchers.une.edu.au/en/publications/using-a-mobile-phone-short-messaging-service-sms-for-irrigation-s-5/). Original Elsevier version-of-record images and any corrections are not independently authenticated here. See the separately retained C86 source-adjudication appendix for arithmetic and source conflicts.

**Advice-specificity and mixed-input finding:** The same [author-uploaded article, pp. 139 and 142](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257018309_Using_a_mobile_phone_Short_Messaging_Service_SMS_for_irrigation_scheduling_in_Australia_-_Farmers%27_participation_and_utility_evaluation) reports that **25%** of respondents accumulated the suggested run-time minutes over several days before irrigating, and **79%** considered multiple information sources before deciding. **The question-specific respondent denominators are not supplied in the inspected passage**, so these percentages cannot be converted to counts or assumed to describe all 72 participants. Crucially, the Discussion states that the DSS **did not prescribe a particular irrigation time or amount**: it supplied water-balance/run-time guidance that growers could combine with other heuristics. A daily advisory message therefore is **not necessarily a discrete, prescriptive irrigation order**. This precludes a binary recommendation-event adherence interpretation without an independently specified actionable target, permissible timing window and tolerance. The findings support mixed-input decision-making, not a measured compliance rate or attributable water saving.
### 3.6 Elia et al. (2022): commercial tomato farms and daily smartphone advice

The original publisher abstract describes six commercial processing-tomato farms, each with DSS-guided and grower-managed plots, and **daily smartphone notifications**. The authors report average seasonal differences of **24% less irrigation water** and **7% less nitrogen**, with no reported yield penalty. These are the authors' seasonal contrasts, not measurements made by the present review. The inspected abstract does not establish whether message receipt, event-by-event compliance, plot allocation, meter calibration, uncertainty and voluntary use in later years were independently documented. The complete methods and tables remain required before a stronger inference. [Original publication](https://doi.org/10.17660/ActaHortic.2022.1335.81).

### 3.7 Baroni et al. (2019): recommendation quality versus implementation

The [Wiley publisher abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ird.2337) distinguishes **direct interviews with end-users** from a **comparative assessment of six scheduling methods**; six methods are not six farms or six independent behavioral observations. **Five of six methods** estimated the *seasonal irrigation amount* consistently, while recommendations differed more in irrigation-season timing and the **number of irrigation events**. Users gave positive feedback, yet the authors qualitatively report that recommendations were rarely implemented. These are three different constructs: seasonal-model agreement, user-reported uptake and actual event-level action. Neither `5/6` nor 'rarely' can be converted to a farmer-level or event-level implementation proportion; the publisher abstract does not supply its event denominator, independent action log or causal field-water-saving effect. The original Wiley page lists supplement `ird2337-sup-0001-Data_S1.docx`, but the supplementary file content was not accessible for verification here. Full methods and the interviews' denominators are needed before extracting quantitative adherence. [Original publication](https://doi.org/10.1002/ird.2337).

### 3.8 Darouich et al. (2024): farmer schedules compared with optimized alternatives

The original post-evaluation addresses 20 center-pivot maize plots in Portugal and directly examines farmer schedules relative to optimized recommendations. Weekly applied irrigation information was collected via technician surveys, with temporary assumptions of compliance when entries were delayed. Modeled evapotranspiration and yield for alternative schedules must not be presented as directly measured causal gains. The publisher's abstract names **2019 and 2020**, while Methods §2.3 and the Results discuss **2019 and 2022**; this is a source-internal year conflict, not two independently verified comparisons. Until clarified, do not attribute any seasonal numerical contrast to an unambiguous second year. This study refutes any novelty claim based solely on comparing advice with farmer practice. [Original publication](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy14010066).

## 5. Discussion: what the evidence does and does not connect

The cases separate three propositions frequently bundled into a single success label. Experimental and commercial plot studies can support **within-study agronomic contrasts**, although assignment, measurement and uncertainty determine causal strength. Messaging and survey studies can support **participation or reported-use claims**, without proving advice-specific irrigation actions. Simulation and optimization can support **scenario potential**, without establishing realized savings. Pooling the Lebanese experimental contrast, Italian commercial-farm contrast and Slovenian modeled 25% scenario as a common water-saving effect would violate their differing designs and denominators.

A prospective reporting template could record six links: (1) recommendation identifier, field and timestamp; (2) delivery and evidence of receipt; (3) independently documented action, including departures from advice; (4) calibrated physical water and crop measurements; (5) comparator, assignment and uncertainty; and (6) later voluntary use or transfer of learned heuristics. The original G/D/R/A shorthand should not be treated as a validated scale, and no retrospective score is computed here. Existing Lorite advisory-comparison, telemetry, Baroni implementation and Ara adoption research already cover major parts of this terrain. The potential benefit of a linked audit trail is **claim transparency**; its novelty and inter-rater reliability remain untested.

Two threats to synthesis deserve special attention. First, repeated plots, farms, respondents, advice events and simulated seasons are not interchangeable independent replicates. Second, multiple publications from a common scheme may share sites or personnel: the Genil–Cabra reports from 2008–2013 show geographic and investigator overlap, although duplicate plot observations have not been proven. Neither source should be counted as an independent replication without inspecting its sampling frame and observation dates.

## 5.1. Proposed, falsifiable source-to-claim recording protocol (not validated)

A prospective record should include: a unique recommendation ID, timestamp and plot/crop; advice specificity/type and any explicit action target; numerical advice (depth, duration or date) and unit; issue, delivery and actual-receipt evidence as separate fields; independently measured action and its timestamp; calibrated water and crop measurements; weather and allocation context; assignment, comparator and farm/season clustering; and later voluntary reuse. For event-level adherence, an observation is an **eligible actionable recommendation-event**, not a farmer, farm or informational message; informational exposure is tracked separately. The accessible source material inspected for this review has not established a complete prospective event ledger; this does not exclude the possibility of uninspected original records.

**Issued-advice estimand and structural nonreceipt rule.** Restricting the denominator to recommendations with *verifiable action linkage* would select on successful follow-up and can inflate implementation. Define the primary population as all prospectively eligible, deduplicated **actionable prescriptive recommendations** (or conditional recommendations with an observed trigger and defined response) **issued** in a prespecified farm/time window (\(N\)); record separate stage counts for successful delivery, verified receipt and measured action. These stage counts need not share the same observational denominator and cannot be used interchangeably. A delivery- or receipt-conditional proportion, if presented, is a secondary estimand with its own explicit population and missingness assumptions.

Classify each eligible issued recommendation exactly once, with a source-backed audit trail: **I** = verified implementation (independently verified receipt followed by time-linked measured action within a prespecified tolerance); **D** = verified departure after verified receipt, comprising either (a) a time-linked measured action outside the prespecified target/window/tolerance or (b) independently established **absence of the prescribed action throughout an adequately observed action window**. A missing sensor record or absent diary is **U**, never evidence of a verified zero action; **B** = *verified nonreceipt* or definitive failed delivery, with no alternate receipt channel and an auditable observation window, so the strict **received-and-implemented** event cannot have occurred; **U** = unresolved or unadjudicable, including unknown receipt, missing action measurement, ambiguous event linkage or unspecified tolerance. A delivery failure alone is not B if another delivery channel may have reached the farmer; classify as U unless nonreceipt is actually established. An irrigation action resembling the advice but occurring without verified receipt does **not** satisfy I. Thus **\(N=I+D+B+U\)**, with nonnegative disjoint counts. Preserve timestamps, channels, late receipt and measurement failures as auxiliary flags without double-counting events.

For **\(N>0\)**, the fraction of issued recommendations satisfying the strict receipt-and-implementation definition is bounded by **\([I/N,(I+U)/N]\)**. The lower endpoint counts only verified implementations; the upper assigns all *unresolved* events, but **not structurally verified nonreceipt B**, to potential implementation. Both are arithmetic identification bounds, **not confidence intervals, empirical adherence estimates or causal effects**. If \(N=0\), no fraction is defined. The **receipt-and-action-resolved conditional fraction** **\(I/(I+D)\)** is reportable only if \(I+D>0\), explicitly conditional on verified receipt and adequately adjudicated action; verified nonreceipt **B** is already resolved but intentionally excluded from this secondary receipt-conditional denominator. This is **not** the fraction of all fully adjudicated issued events; it must not be presented as the issued-population fraction. If no verifiable nonreceipt category can be established, **\(B=0\)** and the upper bound remains conservative, provided the structural-nonreceipt classification is not assumed without evidence. In particular, a self-report of receipt, a server-side delivery flag and a time-linked physical action represent distinct evidentiary links.

**Interpretation limit.** The bound applies to the explicitly operationalized *verified receipt-and-implementation* process, not to the unobservable counterfactual question of whether a farmer would have irrigated similarly without seeing the advice. If the intended outcome is instead *physical agreement with the recommendation regardless of receipt*, the B category is not a structural zero and this tighter upper bound is invalid; the estimand and its bounds must be redefined. Prespecify farm/season clustering, event windows, tolerance, calibration and adjudication rules before field collection. No reviewed paper has been demonstrated, **from the source material inspected here**, to supply every link of this prospective event ledger; this does not assert that uninspected original records do not exist.

Any future causal water-saving claim requires a separately specified counterfactual estimand, assignment mechanism, comparator and uncertainty, with farm/season clustering or repeated-measures dependence addressed. Event-level process proportions, even with complete data, cannot by themselves establish attributable water savings or sustained voluntary use. Independent blinded coding, inter-rater reliability, prespecified sensitivity analyses and prospective validation remain necessary before the template or its classifications could be described as validated. **No numeric adherence, bounds or causal effect is calculated in this review because the required event-level data do not exist here.**

## 5.2. Source-to-claim verification decision rule

For each quantitative claim, archive its original table/figure locator, source version, observation unit, numerator, denominator, comparator, measurement provenance, period and unresolved contradiction. Classify it as *source-reported*, *arithmetically reconstructed*, *modeled*, or *not independently verified*. Do not substitute a reconstructed denominator (for example Cvejić's plausible 35) for a source-authenticated item-response ledger, or treat a conflicting Car transcription as the original publisher table. For source-level disagreements, retain competing figures and block any pooled estimate until the publisher original, correction or author clarification resolves them. This rule is an editorial specification, not a validated instrument or new empirical finding.

## 5.3. Advice specificity and identifiability gate (conceptual, not validated)

A recommendation-delivery-action (G/D/R/A) audit is only interpretable if the **action requested by the message is operationally defined before outcomes are inspected**. Record `advice_type` as: **prescriptive** (explicit target volume/depth/duration and action window), **conditional** (a specified rule or trigger with observable antecedent), or **informational/non-prescriptive** (water balance, indicative run time or contextual guidance without a required irrigation event). This is a proposed editorial taxonomy, **not a validated instrument or a retrospective reclassification of all eight studies**.

For a prescriptive message, adherence may be adjudicated only when a prespecified action target, time window, tolerance, independently documented applied action, and relevant exceptions are available. For a conditional message, verify both the triggering condition and the specified response. For an informational message, **binary adherence is undefined** absent an agreed action rule; instead report verified delivery, receipt, consideration, subsequent decision and measured field outcome as separate constructs. A missing prescriptive target must **not** be coded as farmer noncompliance or as a zero in an adherence denominator.

This matters for Car et al. (2012): although its SMS communicated dripper run-time guidance, the authors explicitly state that the service did not stipulate when or how much to irrigate, and respondents combined the guidance with other information. Their 25% multi-day accumulation and 79% multi-source decision reports lack authenticated item-specific denominators. Neither percentage, the Table 5 'Close' label, nor the 53/72 consideration measure can be transformed into a verified event-specific compliance estimate. The protocol's **recommendation-event denominator is restricted to eligible, operationally prescriptive events**; non-prescriptive advice belongs to a separately reported informational-exposure denominator. This avoids penalizing farmers for discretion expressly allowed by the advisory design.

**Reproducible classification check:** If a message has no explicit action target/window and no trigger-response rule, classify its event-level adherence as `NOT_DEFINED`, not `0` or `1`. If the original wording is unavailable, use `UNKNOWN_ADVICE_TYPE`, not `NONCOMPLIANT`. These are logic safeguards, not observed trial results.

## 5.4. Seasonal recommendation ratios are not event-level fidelity

**Lorite et al. (2012), independently checked at the published abstract level.** The [bibliographic abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/waterr/v26y2012i8p2397-2419.html) defines ARIS LIAS as an **annual relative irrigation supply** comparison of actual irrigation applied with LIAS recommendation over the first five years of advisory service in the Genil–Cabra scheme. The reported crop-level values range from approximately **0.23 (wheat and sunflower) to 0.94 (maize)**, with substantial between-farmer variation. A ratio of 0.23 is **not** '23% of recommendation events followed', nor does 0.94 prove '94% adoption': the numerator and denominator are seasonal water quantities, not counts of messages, deliveries, receipts or implemented actions. A value below one cannot alone distinguish intentional deficit irrigation, allocation constraints, crop-specific strategy, measurement limitations or disregard of advice. The accessible abstract does not resolve those mechanisms or supply a complete event ledger; no causal attribution or numeric adherence rate is drawn here. The full original methods remain a verification gate.

**Baroni et al. (2019), publisher-abstract-level comparison.** The [original Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ird.2337) compares six **methods**, reports five/six broadly consistent *seasonal* water amounts, and identifies differences in season timing and irrigation-event counts; user interviews report rare implementation qualitatively. The six methods are not a denominator of farmers or recommendation events. Agreement in estimated seasonal water volume is neither validation of event scheduling nor proof that farmers acted on advice. Quantitative adherence and independently measured water savings are not identifiable from this abstract.

**Cross-study admissibility rule.** Keep three non-substitutable estimands in separate columns: (A) *seasonal water-quantity ratio* (e.g., ARIS: volume applied/volume recommended, with units and measurement provenance), (B) *recommendation-event process* (eligible actionable advice, verified receipt, time-linked physical action and tolerance; §5.1), and (C) *field-outcome contrast* (physical water/yield under a defined comparator, assignment and uncertainty). No mathematical transformation of A or method agreement into B is justified without original event-level linked data; neither A nor B alone identifies C causally. Lorite and Baroni are **prior methodological comparators**, not additional cases in the purposively selected eight-case corpus. This is a source-to-claim inference correction, **not** a newly measured effect or validated scale.

## 6. Limitations

This purposive eight-case corpus cannot estimate the prevalence of DSS effectiveness or adoption. Full methods for Lorite, Baroni and Elia and the original publisher image of Car's disputed table remain unavailable to this version. Car Table 5's group-4/group-5 prose-versus-table reversal and nonconserving counts prevent water-balance category prevalence extraction; Table 6 influence percentages are quarantined in the source appendix because its 41-count table conflicts with the 40-person prose; neither semantic nor arithmetic checks are independent source validation; Amini F7 source statements and Cvejić respondent denominators are unresolved. Source access, coding and the conceptual reporting template have not undergone independent human dual-review, inter-rater testing or prospective field validation. There is no new dataset, no registered protocol, no pooled effect, no independently observed G/D/R/A sequence and no claim of causal water savings produced by this manuscript. These limitations must remain visible in any later journal-facing version.

## 7. Conclusion

A recommendation delivered, a farmer engaged, an irrigation event implemented and a measured water outcome are distinct evidentiary claims. The eight illustrative cases show why their observational units and source provenance should remain explicit. The contribution of this working review is a consolidated critical comparison and a **proposed, unvalidated source-to-claim reporting discipline**, not a novel empirical effect or a first-in-field adoption framework. Original-source reconciliation, substantive human-author scientific judgment and journal-specific compliance are prerequisites for a final manuscript.
