## Abstract — preliminary, not final
Irrigation decision-support systems (DSS) are increasingly promoted as tools for improving water management, yet evidence that a system produces recommendations is not equivalent to evidence that farmers implement those recommendations or save water in practice. This critical review develops a framework distinguishing field-observed agronomic measurements, independently observed on-farm decisions, farmer self-reports, and modeled counterfactual performance. We illustrate the importance of these distinctions through preliminary readings of eight illustrative primary research articles spanning commercial farms, an experimental field station, advisory-use records, and virtual human–machine scheduling exercises. The studies suggest distinct strengths and limitations of field comparison designs, participant feedback, and virtual decision exercises; their reported outcomes are not interchangeable. We argue for explicit reporting of participant and plot denominators, adoption stages, outcome provenance, measurement uncertainty, and independently documented post-trial reuse. **This is a provisional conceptual abstract, not a synthesis of a completed or exhaustively selected corpus; no review-wide result or publication is claimed.**

## 1. Introduction (original working text)
Reliable irrigation recommendations are not themselves water conservation outcomes. An algorithm may generate a feasible schedule; a field sensor may support an irrigation-state estimate; a farmer may regard advice as useful. Nevertheless, whether water use changes depends on how a recommendation is communicated, interpreted and acted upon, how agronomic and commercial constraints shape the decision, and whether the actual applied irrigation is documented. Consequently, a scientifically credible account of irrigation DSS should distinguish *recommendation validity* from *implementation validity* and *outcome validity*.

A related distinction concerns the unit of evidence. A simulation comparing optimized schedules with historical practices can show that a model prefers a different schedule under its assumptions. It does not directly show that the resulting schedule was installed, followed by farmers, or associated with causal reductions in metered water consumption. Conversely, a field comparison of irrigation depth and crop yield may demonstrate a useful empirical contrast but cannot by itself establish long-term voluntary adoption after a project's active research support has ended. Survey responses about usefulness or participation contribute important social evidence, provided they are not recoded as independently observed irrigation behavior.

This review focuses on **actionable irrigation recommendations delivered to human farmers or farm managers** in real agricultural contexts. Fully automated control without a documented human advisory step and exclusively modeled or virtual implementations are discussed in separate contextual categories rather than merged with farmer-uptake measurements. The objective is to clarify the strength of inference offered by different combinations of measured water outcomes and adoption evidence, thereby informing both future field evaluation and transparent reporting.

### 1.1 What earlier reviews already cover, and the limited contribution proposed here
Any claim that irrigation DSS uptake has never been reviewed would be incorrect. Mabhaudhi and colleagues (2023; DOI 10.1016/j.agwat.2023.108590) mapped **81 agricultural-water-productivity decision-support tools**, reporting that **51% were not readily available publicly** and that application at scale was poorly documented. Umutoni and Samadi (2024; DOI 10.1016/j.agwat.2024.108710) reviewed **16 machine-learning irrigation-scheduling studies**, emphasizing data limitations, uncertainty and the applicability of model performance in real settings. Their work already addresses practical adoption barriers and cannot be ignored as a predecessor.

A closer, accessible full-text comparison is Ofosu-Ampong, Abera, Mesfin and Abate (2025; DOI 10.1007/s44279-025-00190-y), who examined **19 digital agro-advisory platforms** in the Global South and described behavioral typologies and adoption/usage levels. The paper explicitly acknowledges reliance on public developer reports, impact metrics and testimonials, with potential positive reporting bias and limited access to independent user data (its Methods and Results). Its classification of platform subscriber/adopter counts is informative about reach but cannot alone identify whether **an individual irrigation recommendation was implemented or led to independently measured water conservation**. This is a *difference in evidentiary unit*, not evidence that earlier authors ignored adoption or that the present review is uniquely original.

The present critical review therefore proposes a **narrow, testable distinction**: report separately the provenance of advice, verified action, water-use outcomes and counterfactual inference in farmer-facing irrigation decisions. The six-link chain is **an analytical proposal**, not a validated new instrument; its incremental value relative to earlier frameworks must be established by transparent worked examples and source-level criticism. This manuscript is not a new comprehensive tool inventory or a statistical estimate of adoption prevalence. **Full-text verification of Mabhaudhi and Umutoni remains pending**; only their public indexed abstracts/catalogue records have been assessed here.

### 1.2 Strongest prior-art challenge: adoption of DSS versus adoption of learned heuristics
A particularly close predecessor is **Ara, Turner, Harrison, Monjardino, deVoil and Rodriguez (2021; DOI 10.1016/j.agwat.2021.107161)**, an open-access review explicitly devoted to *application, adoption and improvement of decision-support systems in irrigated agriculture*. The review evaluates economic irrigation DSS and distinguishes the uptake of software itself from uptake of learned **decision heuristics**. Crucially, stopping software use may indicate that a farmer has internalized useful advice rather than that the tool has failed. It also discusses intermediaries (advisors or agronomists), end-user feedback, participatory design and uncertainty communication. Thus, any broad claim that the present manuscript introduces the distinction between DSS adoption and changed farmer behavior **would duplicate earlier published thinking**. The proposed incremental contribution is much narrower: a *source-level traceability method* that separates an individual recommendation, objectively verifiable action, field-metered outcomes and scenario-based estimates, while allowing learned heuristics to count as a separate, independently assessed pathway. This distinction is an **unvalidated editorial proposal** until worked examples and author-led interpretation substantiate it. Source: Elsevier publisher DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agwat.2021.107161; accessible publisher-indexed text/abstract and external public full-text link, complete source-table audit pending.

A second substantial overlap is **Raffa and colleagues (2025; DOI 10.1111/ejss.70097)**, an open-access European soil-health DST survey identifying 115 tools. Its Table 3 reports an average **expert-assessed adoption score of 3.1/5** (n=81 ratings for this question), and its Table 4 links adoption ratings to perceived usability and suitability. These are **expert assessments of tools**, not a directly observed count of individual farmers following specific irrigation recommendations. The work already discusses participatory design and farmer adoption, so the present manuscript must neither claim these topics are unstudied nor recast its tool-level ratings as observed farm-level compliance. Source: Wiley original article, Table 3 and sections 3.1.5–3.1.6, https://doi.org/10.1111/ejss.70097. Its primary emphasis is soil-health tools, not a causal chain of irrigation-advice implementation.

### 1.2a Further original-publisher prior-art challenge: technology scope and human-centred AI (C18, 2026-10-25)

**Bounajra, El Guemmat, Mansouri and Akef (2024; DOI 10.1016/j.agwat.2024.108758)** published a full-text open-access *Agricultural Water Management* systematic literature review of **55 primary studies**. Its explicit research questions and extraction Table 6 address evapotranspiration parameters (ETo/Kc), soil physical/chemical/hydrological conditions, sensing and AI techniques, farm-management practices, model limitations and an integrated irrigation-planning framework (see original publisher Methods 3.1/3.5, Results 4.1 and Discussion). Therefore, a claim that this manuscript first connects field characteristics, technical performance and irrigation decisions is untenable. Bounajra's stated extraction dimensions concern technologies, model data, findings and limitations; the present manuscript's *potential* difference is event-level evidence for whether a delivered recommendation was acted upon and whether water was independently metered. **This is a comparison of the explicitly described methods, not proof that none of the 55 cited studies contain such evidence.** Publisher: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agwat.2024.108758 .

**Wei, Xu, Kang and colleagues (2024; DOI 10.1007/s44230-024-00072-4)** published an open-access review specifically about **AI in irrigation, end-user behaviour, adoption, human-centred design and trust**. Its original publisher full text, Introduction, Sections 2.1.2–2.1.3 and 3.3, already discuss farmer needs, feedback, the human–machine relationship and the fact that ceasing software use can reflect successful learning of heuristics (also discussed by Ara 2021). It is therefore **incorrect** to describe farmer-centred irrigation AI, uptake barriers, or the distinction between software use and learned decisions as novel here. The proposed incremental claim-to-evidence audit must demonstrate a reproducible *source-specific* separation of advice delivery, independently observed implementation, metered outcome and counterfactual, rather than restating these established concepts. Publisher: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44230-024-00072-4 .

This manuscript remains **not ready for journal submission** until the human author independently evaluates the comparisons and justifies whether the evidence-chain method adds enough beyond existing reviews. No systematic/scoping screening or independent second-human review is claimed.

**Novelty decision:** This manuscript is **NOT a first review of irrigation DSS adoption, farmer behavior or water savings**. At most, it can provide an explicit and useful *claim-to-evidence audit applied across heterogeneous field and simulation designs*, subject to a rigorous comparative table and author-led critical synthesis. If a prior review already implements an equivalent chain at the recommendation-event level, the novelty claim must be withdrawn or further narrowed.

## 2. Review approach — critical narrative, non-systematic (DRAFT; author must finalize actual selection decisions)
This manuscript is being prepared as a **critical narrative review**, not as a scoping or systematic review. Discovery has included dated, recorded bibliographic searches of Europe PMC and OpenAlex, an AGRICOLA-source subset accessed through Europe PMC, and focused source/DOI checks. A preliminary set of 672 bibliographic-source entries generated 604 machine-matched candidate citations; later exploratory targeted queries recovered two previously missed DOI seed records. These counts describe **retrieval operations, not a systematically screened or final included-study set**. AI-reconstructed index abstracts should be verified against original records before verbatim use; some candidates have no indexed abstract.

The analysis will **purposively choose** relevant original studies to contrast methodological designs, including farmer-facing advisory field studies, adoption and reporting studies, and simulated human–machine scheduling exercises. Any chosen reference must have a verified publisher identity/DOI, accessible research methods and source-anchored outcomes. Relevant earlier reviews should be used to evaluate how much of the conceptual terrain has already been synthesized. The final selection criteria, dates and limits will be described without falsely claiming exhaustive database coverage, independent dual-human screening, PRISMA eligibility counts, meta-analysis, or a quantifiable prevalence of positive intervention results. **The sole intended human author must independently inspect the original scientific evidence, select and meaningfully analyze the final source corpus, revise the scientific arguments and approve this methods description. ### Analytical framework
Evidence is categorized by how it is generated, with separate flags for: **M**, directly measured physical field outcomes; **O**, verifiable operator implementation of a particular recommendation; **S**, participant self-report or attitudes; **P**, prediction, simulation or counterfactual outcome; and **U**, undocumented or irreconcilable provenance. These labels are not a hierarchical statistical score. Mixed studies must retain multiple labels, and validation of a model against measured field inputs is not equivalent to demonstrating successful independent deployment. Adoption stages also remain separate: recommendation exposure; perceived usefulness; intention; self-reported use; objectively observed adherence; repeated use; and post-intervention sustained voluntary use.

## 3. Illustrative methodological contrasts — preliminary source-verified cases
### 3.1 Farmer-facing field comparison in Iran
Amini, Emami and Dehghanisanij (2025, DOI **10.1038/s41598-025-26567-z**) report a participatory irrigation DSS evaluation in the Mahabad Plain, Lake Urmia basin. According to the publisher's methods and results, eight monitored farms or orchards contributed within-farm treatment/control comparisons; authors describe field water-measurement approaches and farmer interviews/workshops. Their descriptions differentiate local reductions, increases and little change in irrigation depth, rather than establishing a single universally positive effect. They also report perceived benefits, which should remain participant appraisals, not metered energy or cost savings.

For a critical synthesis, one should ask whether treatments and customary-control sections were allocated comparably, how water delivery was calibrated and monitored by irrigation technology, and whether changes in crop productivity are reproducible from reported denominators. The original publisher article's Tables 4 and 5 have now been independently audited using published rounded values (Section 13). Table 4 gives F7 water productivity 4.6 versus 6.0 kg/m³, a -1.4 kg/m³ contrast, whereas the article conclusion states -0.1 kg/m³; the Table 5 column labeled water-productivity percentage also fails to reconcile with Table 4 in six non-null farm rows. These reporting inconsistencies are explicitly flagged, not silently corrected, and do not warrant a pooled effect estimate. This study provides evidence of on-farm testing and participation, but **does not by itself verify sustained voluntary adoption across later years**. Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-26567-z; detailed extraction `CYCLE12_AMINI_2025_FULLTEXT_EXTRACTION_NOVELTY_AND_PROVENANCE_2026-10-25.md`.

### 3.2 Farmer-reported use and modeled scenarios in Slovenia
Cvejić and coauthors (2020, DOI **10.3390/agronomy10091238**) describe an advisory service involving daily irrigation recommendations, soil-moisture information and a web interface in the Vipava Valley. The paper refers to 35 interested farms and reports that 83% of farmer respondents said they used the DSS in 2020. Farmer feedback thus contributes social evidence of interaction and reported use; it is **not the same as independently logged adherence or a measured percentage reduction in water use**. The source notes that regular irrigation diary completion was problematic (approximately 91% did not keep diaries consistently during the initial period), complicating direct behavioral verification. Importantly, the publisher Methods state that in **2020 the team replaced/augmented this approach with one-to-one telephone conversations and irrigation-action recording every third week**, improving diary completeness. This remains **researcher-elicited self-report**, not independent automated logs or independently metered recommendation adherence; the 2020 83% use report should be attributed to the correct telephone-interview period. The publisher additionally says 6% (two farmers) were dissatisfied, which is not sufficient to reconstruct the 2020 respondent denominator exactly, and the 2019 statement about 46 advice recipients must not be combined arithmetically with the original 35 farms.

Crucially, the article's **25% projected irrigation-volume reduction and 24% projected energy and CO₂ differences are conditional scenario simulations**, not measured realized savings attributable to actual farmer adoption. An unresolved publisher-text statement referring to "Forty-six farmers" alongside 35 initially interested farms deserves an explicit source-check before any person-based numerator/denominator or adoption calculation is used. Source: https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4395/10/9/1238; detailed extraction `PRIMARY_SOURCE_CVEJIC_2020_FIELD_ADOPTION_PROVENANCE_2026-10-25.md`.

### 3.3 Field inputs and virtual human decisions in Nebraska
Jamal and coauthors (2023, DOI **10.1029/2023WR035810**) combine real field observations for maize irrigation with optimized, modeled schedules and a virtual farmer decision workshop. The two-field comparison illustrates potential scheduling contrasts, while the virtual group exercise elicits choices under study-specific assumptions. Farmer workshop participation, actual field inputs and virtual selection outcomes must be treated as **different evidence units**; a counterfactual optimized schedule cannot be presented as achieved savings from field deployment. The study's documentation differentiates ten workshop attendees from two groups of four in the decision exercise. The group schedules and predicted returns should not be treated as eight independent on-farm trial replications. Source: https://doi.org/10.1029/2023WR035810; extraction `CYCLE10_05_JAMAL_2023_PRIMARY_EXTRACTION.md`.

### 3.4 Experimental-field water savings without demonstrated farmer uptake: Lebanon
Abi Saab, Jomaa, Skaf, Fahed and Todorovic (2019; DOI **10.3390/w11020252**) evaluated the Bluleaf smartphone advisory system in durum wheat during two growing seasons at the **Tal Amara experimental station of the Lebanese Agricultural Research Institute**, not a sample of independently operating commercial farmers. The trial comprised **nine 300-m² plots** in a randomized complete block arrangement: three irrigation treatments (Bluleaf schedule, customary farmer-style schedule, and rainfed) with three plot replicates each. The authors describe treatment-specific valves and flow meters, soil-water monitoring, grain-yield measurements and a comparison of modeled versus observed soil-water depletion. Reported gross irrigation was **270 versus 360 mm** for Bluleaf versus the customary schedule in 2016–2017 and **331 versus 450 mm** in 2017–2018. Those reported seasonal contrasts correspond to **25.0%** and **26.4%** less gross irrigation, respectively, relative to their respective customary comparators; the publisher abstract reports **25.7%** overall and more than 1000 m³/ha saved. The abstract's pooled figure should be retained as the author's reported value rather than recomputed from rounded seasonal numbers without the underlying exact plot-level data.

This study is particularly useful because **physical experimental-field irrigation outcomes and model-performance metrics are available** (M and P), whereas the experimental-station design does **not establish independent farmer acceptance, actual farmer adherence or post-project voluntary use** (O/S not established from the inspected methods). Its randomized plots strengthen a site-level agronomic comparison but cannot be counted as nine independent farms or as evidence of widespread technology adoption. Primary publisher text, sections 2.2.2 and 3.1: https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/11/2/252 . **Full open publisher text inspected 2026-10-25; individual table values/uncertainty still require final author verification.**

### 3.5 Actual advisory engagement, not independently metered water saving: Australia
Car, Christen, Hornbuckle and Moore (2012; DOI **10.1016/j.compag.2012.03.003**) describe an SMS-based irrigation-advice interface tested with **72 irrigators** over a 200-day growing season. In the institutional abstract, **45** users transmitted irrigation/rainfall information for the full season, **13** for half the season, and **14** did not return data. These mutually reported categories sum to 72; **45/72 = 62.5%**, reported in the original abstract as **63%** sustained data-return engagement. This is a useful behavioral proxy for interacting with the system, but it is **not proof that 45 users implemented every recommendation** or that the system saved a quantified volume of water. The separate **80% usefulness** inference incorporates retrospective feedback from some users who stopped, so it must not be substituted for 80% objective adherence. The accessible institutional abstract does not supply independently metered on-farm savings or a controlled comparison.

The study supplies an important contrast to a field-only water-savings experiment: **engagement logs and farmer-reported usefulness can be present without directly verified hydrologic impact**, just as water-saving measurements can exist without farmer adoption. Source: University of New England research portal https://researchers.une.edu.au/en/publications/using-a-mobile-phone-short-messaging-service-sms-for-irrigation-s-5/ and FAO AGRIS https://agris.fao.org/search/es/records/65df408f4c5aef494fe1474f . **Abstract/catalogue-level verification only; obtain original article text before final synthesis.**

### 3.6 Commercial-farm agronomic outcomes with unresolved long-term uptake: Italy
Elia, Lazzizera, La Rotonda and Conversa (2022; DOI **10.17660/ActaHortic.2022.1335.81**) report a **six-commercial-farm** 2018 trial of GesCoN fertigation advice for processing tomato. According to the publisher's abstract, each farm had a DSS-guided plot and a farmer-managed comparison plot; growers received daily smartphone notifications. The authors report an average **24% reduction in seasonal irrigation water volume** and **7% reduction in nitrogen fertilizer**, without an observed effect on marketable or total yield, and favorable farmer feedback. Unlike the Lebanon research-station study, the setting includes commercial growers and management of advice on farms. Nevertheless, a single growing season of researcher-supported use plus favorable perceptions does **not establish multi-season voluntary adoption**; the accessible abstract alone cannot verify measurement calibration, treatment allocation, uncertainty, or plot-level event compliance.

This example contributes a distinct **field outcome + grower feedback** configuration, while its source limitations prohibit pooling the reported 24% with Lebanon's 25.7% or Cvejić's modeled 25% as though all three were measured and exchangeable. Primary abstract: https://www.actahort.org/books/1335/1335_81.htm . **Publisher abstract checked 2026-10-25; full article not yet obtained.**

### 3.7 Directly documented implementation gap in Germany: comparative user survey
Baroni, Drastig, Lichtenfeld, Jost and Claas (2019; DOI **10.1002/ird.2337**) surveyed users and compared **six irrigation-scheduling methods** in Germany. The Wiley publisher abstract explicitly states that recommendations were **rarely implemented**, even though user feedback was positive and systems gave broadly consistent *seasonal* irrigation requirements; larger differences arose in irrigation timing and event counts. This is a particularly strong counterexample to any claim that perceived usefulness, prediction agreement or provision of advice guarantees action. However, the publicly inspected publisher abstract does **not** quantify the exact number of independent farm-level advice events implemented, field-metered water conservation attributable to adherence, or persistence after support ended. Its findings should therefore be coded **S** (reported feedback) and an implementation-gap observation with **O/M not independently verified at event level from the abstract**, not a new water-savings effect size. It also establishes that the uptake gap had been empirically discussed before this review; the present manuscript must not claim to have discovered it. Original publisher: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ird.2337 . **Full text and supplementary methods not yet independently checked; provisional inclusion as an illustrative contrast, not a verified quantitative synthesis.**

### 3.8 Direct farmer-adherence evaluation before the present framework: Portugal
Darouich, Simionesei, Oliveira, Neves and Ramos (2024; DOI **10.3390/agronomy14010066**) conducted an open-access post-evaluation of **IrrigaSys** irrigation advice in southern Portugal, selecting **20 maize center-pivot plots** and comparing farmer irrigation schedules with model-optimized alternatives through multicriteria water-saving and economic indicators. This is **direct prior art on farmer adherence**, not merely general DSS adoption or model accuracy; consequently, this review cannot claim priority for studying farmer-versus-recommendation alignment. Weekly applied-irrigation inputs were obtained through water-board technician surveys, and the system sometimes temporarily assumed compliance when records were delayed. Both the farmer-schedule and optimized-schedule **yield and evapotranspiration outputs were model-derived**, not independently measured crop yields. From published rounded mean irrigation volumes, farmer-versus-model differences are approximately **−9.83%** in 2019 (7,800 versus 8,650 m³/ha) and **+1.79%** in 2022 (7,940 versus 7,800 m³/ha); these are **not causal water savings or event-level adherence fractions**. The original PDF's **abstract says 2020**, whereas its Methods, figures, table and Results use **2022** as the second comparison year. We preserve this source discrepancy without silently correcting the publisher. The authors themselves caution that strict adherence cannot be inferred directly from their multicriteria results. The narrower unvalidated proposition here is whether a recommendation-level source-to-action-to-outcome audit adds reproducible evidentiary discrimination beyond such existing approaches. Full original article and audit: https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy14010066 ; `MR-2026-001_C50_IRRIGASYS_DIRECT_ADHERENCE_PRIOR_ART_FULLTEXT_AUDIT_2026-10-27.md`. **Not added to the original purposive seven-case pilot retroactively; included as an eighth, explicitly later-discovered comparator in this revised narrative.**

## 4. Critical argument: from model validity to implementation validity (working)
These preliminary cases make one methodological problem particularly clear: irrigation DSS research frequently combines more than one observational layer within a single publication. A field trial may contain direct water measurements and subjective farmer ratings. A multi-year initiative may report sustained advisory exposure and simulated scenario potential while lacking independently documented farmer irrigation events. A virtual scheduling exercise may elicit realistic decisions while remaining hypothetical in terms of physical irrigation delivered to the farm.

A useful critical review should therefore map **which layer underwrites each conclusion**, rather than assign an entire paper an undifferentiated "real-world validation" or "adoption" designation. The most demanding water-savings claim would ideally link a documented recommendation, a verified farmer implementation event, field-metered water and agronomic measurements, a transparent comparator and adequate follow-up, with sources of confounding addressed. Failing any such element need not negate the study's narrower contribution: a model can still be useful for system design, and farmer self-reports can still inform usability and implementation barriers. Problems arise when evidence valid for one construct is generalized to another.

### 4.1 A three-way inference mismatch: field outcomes, engagement and scenarios
Across the eight illustrative cases, three fundamentally different propositions are being tested. The Lebanese randomized-plot study and the Italian commercial-farm paired comparison address **how water inputs and agronomic outcomes differed under two implemented management strategies**, subject to their design-specific limitations. The Australian SMS trial addresses **whether farmers continued to return operational information when advice was delivered through a low-friction channel**; the primary accessible record does not demonstrate water conservation. The Slovenian study contains **farmer-reported advisory use alongside conditional modeled savings**, which should not be merged into an observed-water-savings estimate. Amini combines local physical comparisons with participatory assessments; Jamal uses measured field inputs alongside virtual human decision-making. Darouich et al. (2024) directly compare farmer schedules with model-optimized schedules, but its reported ETa and yield contrasts remain model-based and do not establish event-matched independent compliance. These contrasts challenge the assumption that any single reported percentage can represent "DSS success" across studies.

An appropriate comparison matrix therefore needs **separate columns for the measured unit (plot/farm/person/irrigation event), exposure and advice delivery, verified action, water metering or model output, comparator, follow-up horizon, and the source document's evidentiary completeness**. Where original full text is inaccessible, the classification is provisional and the denominator or precision is withheld. Even for a randomized research plot, the independent experimental unit is the plot or block, **not a farmer**. Conversely, frequent farmer data submission may indicate engagement without documenting advice-specific adherence.

### 4.2 A falsifiable reporting standard rather than a retrospective score
The proposed six-link claim-to-evidence chain should be framed as a **prospective reporting template**, not a validated quantitative instrument or universally original taxonomy. Each link has a potential observation and a failure mode: recommendation timestamp and agronomic context; verifiable delivery; actual farmer action; field-measured outcome; credible counterfactual with uncertainty; and repeated voluntary use after support ends. Missing evidence narrows what can be inferred, but is **not evidence of treatment failure**. A later methodological validation could assess inter-rater reproducibility of source classification, prediction of independent field outcomes, and incremental explanatory value compared with existing evaluation/adoption frameworks. No such validation has yet been conducted.

**Interpretation:** For a causal or quasi-causal claim about realized conservation, the unit of analysis should be an identifiable advice event (time, field and recommended volume), its independently observed implementation, a measured irrigation outcome, a valid comparator and follow-up. This criterion is proposed as a *reporting standard*, not an instrument already validated across the literature. Repeated software logins, self-reported usefulness and experimentally enforced irrigation each answer different questions. A further complication from Ara et al. (2021) is that farmers may internalize a rule and stop using the DSS: genuine **learned-heuristic adoption** requires a distinct observational definition and must not be inferred solely from discontinued software use.

## 5. Research implications (provisional)
Future reporting should specify the unit and denominator of each claim: farmer, farm, field, treatment plot, irrigation event or simulated season. Authors should distinguish assignment from actual adherence, document the source and calibration of measured water delivery, preserve negative and heterogeneous outcomes, and explain the environmental or economic conditions assumed in counterfactual analyses. For adoption, actual duration of use and participation after the research team's active support ends deserve explicit measurement. At the publication level, linked data and calculation materials would make discrepancies easier to resolve and improve reader trust.

### B. Proposed original methodological contribution: the claim-to-evidence chain
The review's organizing proposition is that a causal claim about real-world DSS benefit has **five separately observable links**: (1) an advisory recommendation with timestamp and agronomic context, (2) documented delivery/exposure to an identified decision-maker, (3) independently logged farmer action or deviation, (4) independently measured physical field water/agronomic outcomes, and (5) an interpretable counterfactual or comparison accounting for field conditions and uncertainty. Sustained adoption adds a **sixth temporal link**, repeated voluntary use after intervention support ends.

A failure to observe a link does **not** prove the tool failed; it restricts the inference that can be drawn. For example, an interview can substantiate perceived usefulness (link 2 and reported use) but not independently establish link 3; a validated soil-moisture model supports prediction fidelity but does not establish link 4; an optimization scenario describes a possible link 5 without observed link 4. The six-link framework is a **proposed analytical interpretation** that the intended human author must critically review, refine and compare against existing adoption and evaluation frameworks before claiming originality.
