# What Does an Irrigation Recommendation Actually Change? An Evidence-Chain Perspective on Farmer Decisions and Water Outcomes

## Abstract

Irrigation decision-support systems (DSS) may generate plausible schedules without producing independently observed changes in farm management or water use. Existing research has already examined adoption, implementation gaps and farmer learning; therefore, identifying these distinctions is not a novel discovery. This Perspective asks a narrower methodological question: what evidence is necessary to connect a particular irrigation recommendation to a farmer's action and a measured water outcome? We critically juxtapose eight purposively chosen primary studies spanning field comparisons, surveys, advisory services, text-message engagement and virtual decisions. We propose recording six separate links—advice generation, delivery, receipt/interpretation, action, water outcome and counterfactual—using non-exclusive provenance labels for physical measurements, independently verifiable action, self-report, simulation and unresolved evidence. In the reviewed examples, reported user engagement, hypothetical savings and field treatment effects frequently answer different questions. We illustrate how apparent numerical inconsistencies and uncertain denominators should be reported rather than silently repaired. The proposed worksheet is a transparent reporting aid, not a validated scale, systematic review or estimate of the prevalence of nonadherence. Future evaluations should connect timestamped advice to independently recorded irrigation events, calibrated meters, credible comparisons and persistence after researcher support.

## 1. Why an evidence chain is needed

An irrigation scheduling algorithm can be technically accurate yet have little influence on irrigation practice. A grower may ignore a recommendation, adapt it to labor availability or equipment constraints, or learn a rule of thumb and cease using the software. Conversely, a favorable plot-level comparison of irrigation depth may result from direct research supervision rather than autonomous, persistent adoption. These possibilities are scientifically distinct. Claims about **advice quality**, **implementation**, **water use**, and **causal savings** require different observations and denominators.

This is not a newly discovered adoption problem. Lorite and colleagues compared irrigation-advisory recommendations with actual management in southern Spain [1]. Car and colleagues investigated participation in SMS irrigation advice [2]. Baroni and colleagues reported that recommendations from six German scheduling systems were rarely implemented despite generally favorable assessments [3]. Ara and colleagues reviewed adoption of irrigated-agriculture DSS, including the possibility that farmers acquire decision heuristics without continuing to operate the tool [4]. Broad tool inventories and technology reviews have also examined accessibility, adoption barriers and practical field integration [5,6]. A behavioral analysis of digital advisory platforms has already appeared in *Discover Agriculture* [7]. Thus the purpose here is **not** to claim priority for noticing that recommendations and actions differ.

Instead, we propose a more precise unit of scrutiny: the **individual claim** made in an irrigation-advice evaluation. When a paper says that a tool was “used,” does it refer to opening a dashboard, returning sensor data, adjusting a specific irrigation event, or independently verified water savings? The same headline percentage can represent different numerators, time horizons and counterfactuals. The suggested contribution is a falsifiable source-traceability worksheet to help authors, reviewers and readers avoid transferring evidence from one claim to another. Its added value over existing approaches remains to be evaluated empirically.

## 2. Scope and critical approach

Eight primary studies were selected **purposively** to illustrate contrasting evidentiary designs: Lorite [1], Car [2], Baroni [3], Amini [8], Cvejić [9], Jamal [10], Abi Saab [11] and Elia [12]. Selection was based on relevance to farmer-facing irrigation recommendations and diversity of field measurement, interviews, advisory records, modeled scenarios and study limitations. This is a **non-systematic Perspective**, not a comprehensive evidence search, PRISMA review, meta-analysis or representative sample of the literature. No study-level inclusion probabilities, pooled effect sizes or general adoption rates can be estimated from these eight examples.

Our additional source discovery is documented in a dated supplementary research audit, not retroactively presented as the original selection history. We prefer publisher articles and institutional source records, distinguish full-text from abstract-only access and avoid attributing uninspected methods to authors. The Lorite, Baroni and Elia claims discussed below remain **abstract/preview bounded** where complete methods were not independently accessible. The publisher full texts of Ara [4], Bounajra [6] and Ofosu-Ampong [7] were consulted for the narrower prior-art comparison. Mabhaudhi [5] remains supported by an institutional abstract, not independent full-text replication. The eight-study purposive sample was not changed retrospectively.

For each *specific claim*, the suggested non-ordinal provenance tags are **M** (physically measured outcome, with instrument and unit specified), **O** (independently verifiable action following a particular recommendation), **S** (survey, self-report, interface engagement or participant-supplied records), **P** (modelled, hypothetical or virtual outcome) and **U** (unresolved or inadequately documented provenance). More than one tag may apply to a study, and U may qualify an otherwise documented outcome. Absence of O is **not** evidence of refusal to comply. These tags have not undergone inter-rater reliability or external validation and should not be presented as a quality score.

## 3. What eight examples can—and cannot—establish

**Direct comparison of advice and practice.** Lorite and colleagues' Spanish advisory-service evaluation [1] compared irrigation recommendations with actual farmer management across crop types. Importantly, the published bibliographic abstract explicitly defines **ARIS LIAS as actual irrigation applied divided by the LIAS recommendation**, with reported crop-level values of approximately **0.23 for wheat and sunflower** and **0.94 for maize**, and substantial between-farmer heterogeneity (Lorite et al., 2012, DOI 10.1007/s11269-012-0023-3; source: https://ideas.repec.org/a/spr/waterr/v26y2012i8p2397-2419.html, checked 26 October 2026). Therefore even a *quantified actual-versus-recommended irrigation ratio* is established prior art, not an innovation of this Perspective. **ARIS is a ratio of irrigation supply, not the proportion of distinct timestamped recommendations followed**. The same source abstract also reports **Irrigation Water Productivity (IWP)** and **Irrigation Water Benefit (IWB)**: optimal-schedule IWP of about **€0.23/m³** for wheat/sunflower/maize, **€0.53/m³** for cotton/sugar beet and **>€2/m³** for garlic; IWB of about **€0.06/m³** for wheat and **€0.13/m³** for sunflower. Thus even a joint comparison of **advice acceptance and irrigation economics** is prior art, not a novelty claim available to this Perspective (supplementary source audit C43). These abstract-reported historical figures are neither current prices nor independent new measurements. The accessible abstract does not establish event-level matching, independent meter calibration or the observation denominators used for each crop. We cannot infer those methods without inspecting the full paper. Baroni and colleagues' German survey [3] provides a complementary warning: user satisfaction and implementation may diverge. Its qualitative “rarely implemented” finding is supported by the publisher abstract; we do not convert it into an invented percentage of missed recommendations. **Additional original-publisher abstract extraction (Baroni et al., 2019; Wiley DOI 10.1002/ird.2337, rechecked 26 October 2026):** the investigators identified **six irrigation scheduling methods**, interviewed end-users and compared their outputs. **Five of six** methods estimated *seasonal irrigation amount* consistently, whereas **larger differences concerned the irrigation season and number of irrigation events**. These are distinct measurement constructs: approximate agreement on a season's total recommended volume is not evidence of agreement on event timing, frequency or farmer implementation. The publisher abstract provides **no event-level compliance numerator/denominator**, no farmer-level adherence percentage, and no metered causal impact attributable to use. Thus the proposed G/D/R/A reporting framework must specify separate seasonal-total, event-count, timing and implemented-volume comparators instead of conflating them.

**Engagement is not compliance.** In the Australian IrriSatSMS study [2], 72 irrigators received daily messages. The published abstract reports that 45 returned irrigation/rainfall data throughout the season, 13 for about half the season and 14 never returned data (45 + 13 + 14 = 72). These are **engagement-duration groups**, not counts of independently verified adherence to each advice event. A newly inspected **author-uploaded full-text PDF transcription** (Car et al., 2012, p. 140, Table 5; independent source-integrity audit C41) displays six behavior-category counts of **26, 7, 3, 6, 15 and 17**: their sum is **74**, whereas the printed total is **72**. The displayed percentages add to **103.2%**, rather than 100%. The adjoining prose labels Groups 4 and 5 in the opposite order to the extracted table, and its assertion that Groups 1–3 comprise **68%** is inconsistent with the displayed **36/72 = 50%**. These observations concern an **author-uploaded PDF-derived text rendering**, not an independently authenticated publisher typeset-page image; transcription or version differences remain possible. The separately reported abstract engagement-duration groups **45 + 13 + 14 = 72** have different definitions and cannot repair Table 5. We therefore exclude the disputed six-category distribution from quantitative conclusions pending inspection of the original publisher page or author clarification; no corrected count, compliance rate, misconduct or confirmed erratum is inferred.

**Reported adoption and hypothetical conservation are separate endpoints.** The Vipava Valley study by Cvejić and colleagues [9] followed a participatory advisory initiative and reported that 83% of farmers said they used the system in 2020. The publisher describes **35 farms** but also separately says **46 farmers** received emailed advice, without a reconciled respondent-flow table. An independent bounded integer-rounding audit (C40) finds that, **if** all eight published whole-percentage items share one denominator at most 46 and the explicitly stated 6% (two dissatisfied farmers) uses that same base, **35** is the unique mathematically compatible denominator (e.g., 29/35 rounds to 83%). This is a **conditional arithmetic consistency result, not verification of 29 respondents or of a 35-person survey**: farms and farmers are different units, item response bases may differ, and no participant-level records were independently inspected. The 83% remains self-reported use, not verified event-level adherence. The projected reductions of approximately 25% in irrigation volume and 24% in energy use are based on **simulated irrigation strategies**, not an independently metered 25% causal reduction achieved by those respondents. The paper itself discusses the limitations of farmers' irrigation diaries and metering; reporting these caveats does not diminish the value of its participatory evidence.

**Field treatment contrasts do not establish independent adoption.** Amini and colleagues' eight-field comparison [8] reports heterogeneous physical outcomes. **New original-publisher full-method audit (C42):** in the 2021–2022 study, eight monitored farms/orchards were split into conventional-control and DSS-treatment sections. Surface irrigation was measured with WSC flumes and flow hydrographs, sprinkler discharge with timed collection into 20-litre containers, drip discharge with graduated collection, and soil moisture gravimetrically at 0–30 and 30–60 cm. The authors state that farmers *attempted* to implement DSS schedules while adjusting irrigation dates and times to field conditions. These methods support a measured seasonal outcome (**M**) and a described treatment protocol, but **not** an independently verified event-by-event recommendation/action denominator (**O**). Model agreement with CROPWAT (average seasonal-depth difference reported below ±7%) is also not proof of event adherence. The F7 orchard treatment is described as applying approximately 292.7 mm more irrigation water than its comparator, rather than a universal reduction. The publisher's own F7 water-productivity reporting is internally inconsistent: the tabulated treatment and control figures (4.6 and 6.0 kg/m³) imply −1.4 kg/m³, while the accompanying results narrative separately describes decreases of **1.3 kg/m³** (F7 paragraph) and **1.4 kg/m³** (later F7 comparison), and the conclusion describes **0.1 kg/m³**. These conflicting source statements were checked directly against the publisher's open HTML on 26 October 2026 (Amini et al., 2025, DOI 10.1038/s41598-025-26567-z; Water productivity and crop yield / Conclusion). We retain the original numerical discrepancy without silently choosing a preferred estimate. This is an unresolved source-level inconsistency, **not** a corrected measurement, and no F7 value should be generalized into an average DSS benefit. Abi Saab and colleagues [11] report a measured field comparison under Mediterranean conditions, including a 25.7% water-saving contrast in a specific evaluation. That is evidence about the studied field design, not evidence that unrelated farmers subsequently adopted the tool without support.

**Virtual decisions are not field behavior.** Jamal and colleagues [10] combine field information and model-based irrigation scheduling with a human–machine interaction component. A farmer's choices in a **virtual** irrigation task provide evidence about decisions under that task's conditions, not independent verification of implementation on a working farm. The commercial-farm GesCoN report by Elia and colleagues [12] offers a more substantive field comparator than our earlier summary conveyed. Its **original ISHS publisher abstract** describes **six commercial processing-tomato farms** in 2018, each with a DSS-managed plot and a plot under empirical grower practice, and reports **mean seasonal irrigation-volume reduction of 24% and nitrogen-application reduction of 7%**, without a reported reduction in total or marketable yield (https://www.actahort.org/books/1335/1335_81.htm, checked 26 October 2026). These are reported **within-study plot-management contrasts**, not an independently verified count of each recommendation received or followed, nor proof of persistent autonomous adoption after support. The abstract alone does not establish the allocation protocol, meter calibration, sampling uncertainty or generalizable causal effect; we do not infer these from the percentages.

## 3A. Scope and prior reporting frameworks (critical novelty test)

A six-stage reporting chain is not new simply because it is explicitly enumerated. The published TIDieR intervention-reporting checklist [13] already separates planned versus actual fidelity, including intervention dose and implementation. Carroll et al. [14] previously distinguished adherence, exposure and participant responsiveness. This Perspective therefore cannot claim a first or validated fidelity reporting framework. Its **proposed, untested irrigation-specific adaptation** is to connect each timestamped, plot-specific recommendation to an independently verifiable irrigation event and to label three distinct denominators: advice events emitted, recommendations actually received and irrigation actions observed. A physically measured irrigation contrast still requires an appropriate control before a claim of causal savings is warranted.

Independent 2026 evidence further constrains novelty. Al-Kilani et al. [15] interviewed and visited 45 Jordanian farms and found barriers to using scientific irrigation parameters. Zhu and Feng [16] used Q-methodology with 45 Chinese smallholders to study post-installation adaptation and selective use of water-saving irrigation technologies. The latter is not necessarily a study of individual human-facing DSS recommendations; both works are **contextual prior art**, not additions to our eight purposively selected examples. We cite their accessible publisher information without treating them as proof of event-level adherence.

Three more independently checked reviews narrow the contribution further. Pronti, Auci and Berbel [17] systematically classify determinants of adoption of irrigation water-conservation technologies in **36** econometric studies selected from **1,027** screened records; that is strong prior art for adoption-factor taxonomies, but their adoption-of-technology estimand is not a per-message implementation denominator. Belfiore and Viaggi [18] reviewed **42** agricultural-water mathematical-programming studies from **438** candidate records and highlight weak representation of behavioral heterogeneity in policy models. This rules out any claim to first identify a model–behavior gap, but model heterogeneity is not itself a measured irrigation advice-event outcome. Liu and colleagues [19] review smart-irrigation decision methods, including the call for closed-loop designs, and thus predate a generic claim that feedback and operational integration are new. The accessible publisher text does not demonstrate that their review validates event-specific human adoption. These **three contextual reviews are not part of the eight purposively selected primary cases**; they serve only as novelty challenges. The Pronti source was checked against bibliographic abstract and open institutional record, not an independently audited full-method extraction.

## 3B. A direct agricultural fidelity comparator overlooked by the initial eight-case frame

A **directly relevant empirical predecessor** is the *IrrigaSys* post-evaluation by Darouich and colleagues [20], not merely a generic adoption review. The 2024 study evaluated **farmers' adherence to weekly irrigation recommendations** in southern Portugal, using 20 maize plots, 2019 and a comparison season, and a multicriteria comparison of actual farmer irrigation management with model-optimized schedules. The publisher's accessible methods explain that **actual irrigation depths were gathered in weekly surveys by local water-board technical staff**, while the DSS issued weekly plot-level reports; when irrigation data were delayed, model updates sometimes temporarily assumed prior advice was followed. These are valuable, unusually close predecessors for *recommendation-versus-action linkage*. However, reported application collected via technicians is **not automatically an independently calibrated meter reading**, and the reported multicriteria utility is not a direct count of all timestamp-matched advice events implemented. The accessible publisher article inconsistently identifies the comparison season as **2020 in the abstract** and **2022 in the data-selection description**; we therefore do not assert a unique second year here without a source correction [20].

Tapsuwan and colleagues [21] had already evaluated a model-based irrigation advisory bulletin with pre/post-trial end-user feedback in Spain, documenting differences between expected and actual information needs. Thus neither iterative user feedback nor assessment after implementation is an original idea here. Both studies are **additional contextual predecessors**, not retrospectively selected members of the eight illustrative cases. A further direct predecessor is the 2025 **MAGDA** irrigation-advisory study by Lagasio and colleagues [22]. It explicitly compares model-derived irrigation recommendations with irrigation quotas **actually applied at farm level under a traditional management method**, while cautioning that the two approaches are not fully comparable. That comparison is **not** evidence that each farmer received or followed a specific MAGDA advice event, nor a validated measurement of causal savings. It nevertheless further defeats any broad claim that published irrigation research has never compared recommended water applications with observed or reported farm practice. The study's sensor-rich meteorological validation must not be misreported as an independent, timestamp-matched validation of farmers' advice adherence.

The remaining contribution, if any, is **not** first documentation of advice–action differences or first linkage of farmer practice to DSS recommendations. It is a proposed *source-provenance and denominator discipline* for **each individual claim**, including how self-reported irrigation depths, physically calibrated measurements and hypothetical schedules should be differentiated. This incremental proposal has not been validated and may be judged too limited for publication.

## 4. A six-link reporting proposal

**Specificity versus prior art.** Ara [4] explicitly excluded daily/real-time irrigation scheduling from its economic-DSS review while already recognizing adoption of learned heuristics. Bounajra [6] extracted field technology, methods and limitations from 55 studies. Ofosu-Ampong [7] classified 19 platforms using subscriber/adopter bands, not independently metered irrigation events. The possible increment here is the claim-specific denominator and causal-inference gate, not an original discovery about adoption; incremental value remains unvalidated.

We suggest that future evaluations report the following links **separately**, with a source, unit, denominator, observation window and uncertainty statement for each:

1. **Advice generation:** the timestamp, plot, crop, algorithm version and proposed depth/duration, including uncertainty and agronomic constraints.
2. **Advice delivery:** evidence that a recommendation was actually transmitted, not merely computed by software.
3. **Receipt and interpretation:** whether a grower accessed and understood the recommendation; a click, SMS return or interview may provide S evidence, not automatically O.
4. **Implementation:** the actual irrigation event and its timing/depth, recorded independently when possible, and linked to the advice event. Modified advice should be recorded as adaptation rather than automatically coded as failure.
5. **Physical outcome:** calibrated metering, soil-water or yield observations, with units and measurement uncertainty. Irrigation depth is not interchangeable with irrigation volume, yield or water productivity.
6. **Counterfactual attribution:** the comparison and assumptions required to attribute a change to advice (randomization, a credible control or a carefully bounded quasi-experiment); metering alone does not establish the counterfactual.

**Operational denominator specification (proposed, unvalidated).** For a prespecified window, distinguish the number of distinct applicable plot-specific recommendations generated (G), those with documented successful delivery (D), those with documented receipt (R), and those with a separately verifiable irrigation action matched to the recommendation under an explicit time/plot rule (A). Report D/G, R/G and A/G with source, missingness and units; conditional A/R is meaningful only on the jointly linkable subset, because an observed action does not prove receipt was logged. Record superseded recommendations, many-to-many action matches, farmer adaptation and missing receipt separately. These are **documentation fractions, not validated adherence probabilities or causal water savings**. A full proposed event-key schema, provenance classes, missingness rules and a fair TIDieR/Carroll-informed active-comparator assessor trial are specified in the companion research protocol `C37_PROPOSED_EVENT_DENOMINATOR_AND_ACTIVE_COMPARATOR_PROTOCOL_2026-10-26.md`; **no data have been collected to validate it**.

**Constructive non-identifiability example (hypothetical, not observed data).** Suppose a plot receives two recommendations, each for 10 mm, on days 1 and 8. Four entirely hypothetical actual schedules have the **same 20 mm seasonal total**: (i) 10 mm on days 1 and 8; (ii) one 20 mm event on day 4; (iii) 5 mm on day 1 and 15 mm on day 8; and (iv) 10 mm on days 2 and 9. All yield a seasonal applied/recommended depth ratio of **1.0**, but differ respectively in event count, matched-event dose and/or timing under any explicitly specified event-matching tolerance. Thus the mapping from an event history to its seasonal total is **many-to-one**: neither perfect event-level adherence nor advice-induced savings is identifiable from that total alone. Even a fully verified event history cannot identify causal savings without an appropriate counterfactual. This is an elementary logical counterexample offered for measurement clarity, **not** a new statistical estimator, empirical finding, or claim of priority over Lorite, Baroni or fidelity-reporting prior art.

**Cross-cutting follow-up:** record persistence after researcher or extension support ends with an explicit observation horizon. This is a time dimension of all six domains, not a seventh causal link.

These links form a **reporting chain, not a causal model**. For example, delivery without evidence of receipt does not prove that a recommendation was ignored. An observed reduction in applied water without an appropriate comparator does not establish that the DSS caused it. Nor does a grower's decision to use a learned heuristic rather than the app imply failed adoption [4]. The worksheet should preserve multiple outcomes rather than collapse them into a single “DSS success” percentage.

**Direct empirical overlap.** Lorite [1] had already quantified actual irrigation relative to advisory recommendations using ARIS LIAS, including crop-specific ratios, so even a generic *recommended-versus-applied denominator* is prior art. The IrrigaSys evaluation [20] also compares plot-level farmer irrigation practice with weekly DSS schedules, although its applied-depth data were collected through technicians' weekly surveys and its main outcome is a multicriteria comparison rather than an independently metered event-by-event adherence fraction. The Tapsuwan bulletin evaluation [21] also predates this manuscript's discussion of post-implementation user feedback. Accordingly, the only potentially incremental reporting feature is the explicit *claim-specific unit, provenance class and matched event denominator*, not recommendation–action comparison itself.

**Prior-art crosswalk and narrow incremental question.** TIDieR [13] already asks about planned and actual intervention fidelity, while Carroll [14] already separates adherence, exposure and participant response; therefore the six links above are an *irrigation reporting adaptation*, not a novel fidelity theory. Pronti [17] concerns *determinants of adoption of water-saving technologies*; Belfiore and Viaggi [18] concern *heterogeneous agricultural-water policy/optimization models*; Liu et al. [19] concern *algorithmic smart-irrigation decisions and closed-loop design*. None of these contextual records alone supplies a verified denominator of **distinct applicable irrigation advice events matched to independently observed plot irrigation actions**. Yet absence of that evidence in the cited review abstracts is **not** proof that no published primary study has ever implemented such a denominator. The remaining research question is whether adding a source-linked event key and separate numerators/denominators to an established fidelity checklist reduces unsupported inferences in a prespecified blinded extraction exercise. This comparison has not been conducted.

An explicit example illustrates why denominators matter. The Car SMS study [2] has 72 message recipients and 45 full-season data returners. The estimand “fraction of recipients returning data all season” is 45/72; the estimand “fraction of advice events independently followed” would require an entirely different numerator and the count of applicable recommendations. The value **45/72 = 62.5%** refers only to all-season data-return engagement. It cannot estimate recommendation-specific adherence because that would require an entirely different denominator (applicable advice events) and verified action records. Similarly, Cvejić's reported 83% system use [9] is not a direct estimate of the fraction of irrigation events matching advice, while its simulated 25% reduction concerns a modeled strategy rather than observed use.

## 5. How to test whether the worksheet adds value

The central claim of this Perspective is **conditional and testable**: explicit claim-level provenance might reduce overinterpretation compared with conventional narrative descriptions. That proposition requires validation, not merely a convincing illustration. A suitable future study would preregister a sampling frame of published irrigation-advice claims, stratify by source-access level and randomly assign independent domain assessors to **a TIDieR/Carroll-informed extraction form versus that same form augmented with timestamped irrigation-advice-event linkage, three explicit denominators and a causal-contrast field**. This active-comparator design isolates the claimed incremental reporting value rather than contrasting a fully specified form with an artificially weak narrative baseline. The **primary endpoint** would be the fraction of extracted claims with unsupported inferences, determined by blinded adjudication against the original source and a prespecified rulebook. Secondary endpoints would be assessor agreement, missingness detection and extraction time. Analysis should account for clustering by assessor and source paper. Sample size requires an honest pilot estimate before recruitment. A null or unfavorable outcome would count against the proposed worksheet. Before use, the design should be checked against existing risk-of-bias and reporting frameworks rather than assuming a new checklist is intrinsically superior.

A second empirical evaluation should follow farmers across seasons, combining timestamped advice, opt-in interaction logs, meter-verified irrigation events, weather/soil data and post-project follow-up. Privacy protections and consent are essential because detailed farm decision logs may be commercially sensitive. Sampling should distinguish adopters, non-adopters and farmers who have internalized heuristics. This design could reveal when software disengagement is compatible with improved management, a possibility emphasized in earlier adoption literature [4]. Neither experiment has been performed in the present Perspective.

## 6. Limits and implications

The eight examples were chosen for methodological contrast, not by a systematic sampling frame. Several source claims are abstract-bounded; the original Car Table 5 page image has not been verified; and Amini's published numerical inconsistency is not independently resolved. We cannot infer the prevalence of nonadherence, the average benefit of DSS, the superiority of any particular tool or the causal contribution of a proposed reporting form. We also cannot claim to have established the worksheet's reliability, sensitivity or incremental novelty beyond prior reviews [4–7]. The reviewed full texts restrict the candidate distinction to **claim-specific recommendation-event denominators and causal-inference gates**. The 2024–2026 reviews [17–19] further eliminate generic claims of priority in adoption determinants, model–behavior integration and smart-irrigation closed-loop design. Lorite's published ARIS LIAS actual/recommended irrigation ratios [1], the direct IrrigaSys post-evaluation [20] and the earlier advisory-bulletin user-feedback evaluation [21] constrain the narrower claim that linking recommendations to practice or constructing an applied/recommended ratio is original. The proposed additional value remains an **untested hypothesis**, not a finding. The material supports a narrower conclusion: different irrigation-advice studies legitimately measure **different constructs**, and a reviewer should not substitute one construct for another.

For authors, the practical recommendation is to label each percentage with its numerator, denominator, observation unit and measurement method. For editors, the corresponding recommendation is to ask whether a reported outcome is a scenario, participant report, independently recorded event or physically measured endpoint. For future investigators, the challenge is to connect these layers without assuming that compliance is always desirable: a farmer may reasonably modify a recommendation because of equipment, water allocation, labor, market or crop conditions. Transparent evidence classification should make such mechanisms easier to study rather than judging farmers by algorithmic obedience.

## 7. Conclusion

Irrigation DSS evaluation requires precision about what was recommended, communicated, chosen, implemented and measured. Previous researchers have already recognized the adoption problem. The proposed six-link worksheet offers a way to expose claim-level gaps, not a validated discovery or a new estimate of water savings. Its utility should be established by independent, preregistered comparison with existing review practice and by field studies that observe advice, action and outcomes together.
