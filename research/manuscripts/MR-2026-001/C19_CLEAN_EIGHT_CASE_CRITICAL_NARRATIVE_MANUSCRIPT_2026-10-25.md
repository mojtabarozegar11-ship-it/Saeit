# From Irrigation Recommendations to Field Outcomes: A Critical Review of Validation Evidence and Farmer Uptake under Water Scarcity
**Article type:** Critical Narrative Review (purposive, non-systematic).  
**Proposed sole human author:** Mojtaba Roozegar (byline, affiliation, substantive intellectual contribution and final approval require author verification).  
**Editorial status:** Substantive AI-assisted research manuscript; not submitted, accepted or published.  

## Abstract
**Background:** A technically accurate irrigation decision-support system (DSS) need not be implemented by farmers, and simulated irrigation savings are not equivalent to measured reductions in applied water. **Aim:** To critically examine the units of observation and strength of inference used when irrigation advisory studies describe field performance and farmer uptake. **Approach:** This purposive, non-systematic critical narrative review compares eight contrasting primary studies from field plots, commercial farms, advisory programmes, farmer–system interactions and simulations. We distinguish physical measurement (M), independently verifiable recommendation-specific action (O), self-report or engagement (S), modeled performance (P) and unresolved provenance (U). **Critical observations:** In one eight-site field study, a published table indicates that irrigation under the DSS treatment increased rather than decreased at one site; some water-productivity figures do not reconcile across its tables and conclusion. A Slovenian advisory study reports use by farmers but its headline water and energy savings are modeled scenarios. A 72-user Australian SMS trial documents return of operational messages and qualitative advice-versus-reported-irrigation patterns, not independently metered adherence to every recommendation. An earlier five-year Spanish irrigation-advisory evaluation already compared actual irrigation with recommendations using crop-specific supply ratios, challenging any claim that recommendation–action comparisons are new. **Interpretation:** Evidence must be tied to the specific claim, unit, time horizon, measurement process and counterfactual; missing evidence should not be coded as nonadherence. **Conclusion:** A transparent claim-to-evidence worksheet can improve reporting discipline, but is a proposed editorial method, not a validated metric or an unprecedented adoption framework. No pooled savings or adoption prevalence is inferred.

## 1. Introduction
Reliable irrigation recommendations are not themselves water conservation outcomes. An algorithm may generate a feasible schedule; a field sensor may support an irrigation-state estimate; a farmer may regard advice as useful. Nevertheless, whether water use changes depends on how a recommendation is communicated, interpreted and acted upon, how agronomic and commercial constraints shape the decision, and whether the actual applied irrigation is documented. Consequently, a scientifically credible account of irrigation DSS should distinguish *recommendation validity* from *implementation validity* and *outcome validity*.

A related distinction concerns the unit of evidence. A simulation comparing optimized schedules with historical practices can show that a model prefers a different schedule under its assumptions. It does not directly show that the resulting schedule was installed, followed by farmers, or associated with causal reductions in metered water consumption. Conversely, a field comparison of irrigation depth and crop yield may demonstrate a useful empirical contrast but cannot by itself establish long-term voluntary adoption after a project's active research support has ended. Survey responses about usefulness or participation contribute important social evidence, provided they are not recoded as independently observed irrigation behavior.

This review focuses on **actionable irrigation recommendations delivered to human farmers or farm managers** in real agricultural contexts. Fully automated control without a documented human advisory step and exclusively modeled or virtual implementations are discussed in separate contextual categories rather than merged with farmer-uptake measurements. The objective is to clarify the strength of inference offered by different combinations of measured water outcomes and adoption evidence, thereby informing both future field evaluation and transparent reporting.

### 1.1 What earlier reviews already cover, and the limited contribution proposed here
Any claim that irrigation DSS uptake has never been reviewed would be incorrect. Mabhaudhi and colleagues (2023; DOI 10.1016/j.agwat.2023.108590) mapped **81 agricultural-water-productivity decision-support tools**, reporting that **51% were not readily available publicly** and that application at scale was poorly documented. Umutoni and Samadi (2024; DOI 10.1016/j.agwat.2024.108710) reviewed **16 machine-learning irrigation-scheduling studies**, emphasizing data limitations, uncertainty and the applicability of model performance in real settings. Their work already addresses practical adoption barriers and cannot be ignored as a predecessor.

A closer, accessible full-text comparison is Ofosu-Ampong, Abera, Mesfin and Abate (2025; DOI 10.1007/s44279-025-00190-y), who examined **19 digital agro-advisory platforms** in the Global South and described behavioral typologies and adoption/usage levels. The paper explicitly acknowledges reliance on public developer reports, impact metrics and testimonials, with potential positive reporting bias and limited access to independent user data (its Methods and Results). Its classification of platform subscriber/adopter counts is informative about reach but cannot alone identify whether **an individual irrigation recommendation was implemented or led to independently measured water conservation**. This is a *difference in evidentiary unit*, not evidence that earlier authors ignored adoption or that the present review is uniquely original.

The present critical review therefore proposes a **narrow, testable distinction**: report separately the provenance of advice, verified action, water-use outcomes and counterfactual inference in farmer-facing irrigation decisions. The six-link chain is **an analytical proposal**, not a validated new instrument; its incremental value relative to earlier frameworks must be established by transparent worked examples and source-level criticism. This manuscript is not a new comprehensive tool inventory or a statistical estimate of adoption prevalence. **Full-text verification of Mabhaudhi and Umutoni remains pending**; only their public indexed abstracts/catalogue records have been assessed here.

### 1.2 Strongest prior-art challenge: adoption of DSS versus adoption of learned heuristics
A particularly close predecessor is **Ara, Turner, Harrison, Monjardino, deVoil and Rodriguez (2021; DOI 10.1016/j.agwat.2021.107161)**, an open-access review explicitly devoted to *application, adoption and improvement of decision-support systems in irrigated agriculture*. The review evaluates economic irrigation DSS and distinguishes the uptake of software itself from uptake of learned **decision heuristics**. Crucially, stopping software use may indicate that a farmer has internalized useful advice rather than that the tool has failed. It also discusses intermediaries (advisors or agronomists), end-user feedback, participatory design and uncertainty communication. Thus, any broad claim that the present manuscript introduces the distinction between DSS adoption and changed farmer behavior **would duplicate earlier published thinking**. The proposed incremental contribution is much narrower: a *source-level traceability method* that separates an individual recommendation, objectively verifiable action, field-metered outcomes and scenario-based estimates, while allowing learned heuristics to count as a separate, independently assessed pathway. This distinction is an **unvalidated editorial proposal** until worked examples and author-led interpretation substantiate it. Source: Elsevier publisher DOI https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agwat.2021.107161; accessible publisher-indexed text/abstract and external public full-text link, complete source-table audit pending.

A second substantial overlap is **Raffa and colleagues (2025; DOI 10.1111/ejss.70097)**, an open-access European soil-health DST survey identifying 115 tools. Its Table 3 reports an average **expert-assessed adoption score of 3.1/5** (n=81 ratings for this question), and its Table 4 links adoption ratings to perceived usability and suitability. These are **expert assessments of tools**, not a directly observed count of individual farmers following specific irrigation recommendations. The work already discusses participatory design and farmer adoption, so the present manuscript must neither claim these topics are unstudied nor recast its tool-level ratings as observed farm-level compliance. Source: Wiley original article, Table 3 and sections 3.1.5–3.1.6, https://doi.org/10.1111/ejss.70097. Its primary emphasis is soil-health tools, not a causal chain of irrigation-advice implementation.

### 1.3 Earlier technology and human-centred irrigation reviews

**Bounajra, El Guemmat, Mansouri and Akef (2024; DOI 10.1016/j.agwat.2024.108758)** published a full-text open-access *Agricultural Water Management* systematic literature review of **55 primary studies**. Its explicit research questions and extraction Table 6 address evapotranspiration parameters (ETo/Kc), soil physical/chemical/hydrological conditions, sensing and AI techniques, farm-management practices, model limitations and an integrated irrigation-planning framework (see original publisher Methods 3.1/3.5, Results 4.1 and Discussion). Therefore, a claim that this manuscript first connects field characteristics, technical performance and irrigation decisions is untenable. Bounajra's stated extraction dimensions concern technologies, model data, findings and limitations; the present manuscript's *potential* difference is event-level evidence for whether a delivered recommendation was acted upon and whether water was independently metered. **This is a comparison of the explicitly described methods, not proof that none of the 55 cited studies contain such evidence.** Publisher: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.agwat.2024.108758 .

**Wei, Xu, Kang and colleagues (2024; DOI 10.1007/s44230-024-00072-4)** published an open-access review specifically about **AI in irrigation, end-user behaviour, adoption, human-centred design and trust**. Its original publisher full text, Introduction, Sections 2.1.2–2.1.3 and 3.3, already discuss farmer needs, feedback, the human–machine relationship and the fact that ceasing software use can reflect successful learning of heuristics (also discussed by Ara 2021). It is therefore **incorrect** to describe farmer-centred irrigation AI, uptake barriers, or the distinction between software use and learned decisions as novel here. The proposed incremental claim-to-evidence audit must demonstrate a reproducible *source-specific* separation of advice delivery, independently observed implementation, metered outcome and counterfactual, rather than restating these established concepts. Publisher: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s44230-024-00072-4 .

**Adversarial finding:** These two additional prior reviews materially narrow novelty. This manuscript remains **not ready for journal submission** until the human author independently evaluates the comparisons and justifies whether the evidence-chain method adds enough beyond existing reviews. No systematic/scoping screening or independent second-human review is claimed.

**Novelty decision:** This manuscript is **NOT a first review of irrigation DSS adoption, farmer behavior or water savings**. At most, it can provide an explicit and useful *claim-to-evidence audit applied across heterogeneous field and simulation designs*, subject to a rigorous comparative table and author-led critical synthesis. If a prior review already implements an equivalent chain at the recommendation-event level, the novelty claim must be withdrawn or further narrowed.

## 2. Review approach: purposive critical narrative comparison
This manuscript is being prepared as a **critical narrative review**, not as a scoping or systematic review. Discovery has included dated, recorded bibliographic searches of Europe PMC and OpenAlex, an AGRICOLA-source subset accessed through Europe PMC, and focused source/DOI checks. A preliminary set of 672 bibliographic-source entries generated 604 machine-matched candidate citations; later exploratory targeted queries recovered two previously missed DOI seed records. These counts describe **retrieval operations, not a systematically screened or final included-study set**. AI-reconstructed index abstracts should be verified against original records before verbatim use; some candidates have no indexed abstract.

The analysis will **purposively choose** relevant original studies to contrast methodological designs, including farmer-facing advisory field studies, adoption and reporting studies, and simulated human–machine scheduling exercises. Any chosen reference must have a verified publisher identity/DOI, accessible research methods and source-anchored outcomes. Relevant earlier reviews should be used to evaluate how much of the conceptual terrain has already been synthesized. The final selection criteria, dates and limits will be described without falsely claiming exhaustive database coverage, independent dual-human screening, PRISMA eligibility counts, meta-analysis, or a quantifiable prevalence of positive intervention results. **The sole intended human author must independently inspect the original scientific evidence, select and meaningfully analyze the final source corpus, revise the scientific arguments and approve this methods description. This step is NOT yet complete.**

### Analytical framework
Evidence is categorized by how it is generated, with separate flags for: **M**, directly measured physical field outcomes; **O**, verifiable operator implementation of a particular recommendation; **S**, participant self-report or attitudes; **P**, prediction, simulation or counterfactual outcome; and **U**, undocumented or irreconcilable provenance. These labels are not a hierarchical statistical score. Mixed studies must retain multiple labels, and validation of a model against measured field inputs is not equivalent to demonstrating successful independent deployment. Adoption stages also remain separate: recommendation exposure; perceived usefulness; intention; self-reported use; objectively observed adherence; repeated use; and post-intervention sustained voluntary use.

## 3. Illustrative methodological contrasts and evidentiary limits
### 3.1 Farmer-facing field comparison in Iran
Amini, Emami and Dehghanisanij (2025, DOI **10.1038/s41598-025-26567-z**) report a participatory irrigation DSS evaluation in the Mahabad Plain, Lake Urmia basin. According to the publisher's methods and results, eight monitored farms or orchards contributed within-farm treatment/control comparisons; authors describe field water-measurement approaches and farmer interviews/workshops. Their descriptions differentiate local reductions, increases and little change in irrigation depth, rather than establishing a single universally positive effect. They also report perceived benefits, which should remain participant appraisals, not metered energy or cost savings.

For a critical synthesis, one should ask whether treatments and customary-control sections were allocated comparably, how water delivery was calibrated and monitored by irrigation technology, and whether changes in crop productivity are reproducible from reported denominators. The original publisher article's Tables 4 and 5 have now been independently audited using published rounded values (see the source-linked calculation audit cited below). Table 4 gives F7 water productivity 4.6 versus 6.0 kg/m³, a -1.4 kg/m³ contrast, whereas the article conclusion states -0.1 kg/m³; the Table 5 column labeled water-productivity percentage also fails to reconcile with Table 4 in six non-null farm rows. These reporting inconsistencies are explicitly flagged, not silently corrected, and do not warrant a pooled effect estimate. This study provides evidence of on-farm testing and participation, but **does not by itself verify sustained voluntary adoption across later years**. Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-26567-z; detailed extraction `CYCLE12_AMINI_2025_FULLTEXT_EXTRACTION_NOVELTY_AND_PROVENANCE_2026-10-25.md`.

### 3.2 Farmer-reported use and modeled scenarios in Slovenia
Cvejić and coauthors (2020, DOI **10.3390/agronomy10091238**) describe an advisory service involving daily irrigation recommendations, soil-moisture information and a web interface in the Vipava Valley. The paper refers to 35 interested farms and reports that 83% of farmer respondents said they used the DSS in 2020. Farmer feedback thus contributes social evidence of interaction and reported use; it is **not the same as independently logged adherence or a measured percentage reduction in water use**. The source notes that regular irrigation diary completion was problematic (approximately 91% did not keep diaries consistently during the initial period), complicating direct behavioral verification. Importantly, the publisher Methods state that in **2020 the team replaced/augmented this approach with one-to-one telephone conversations and irrigation-action recording every third week**, improving diary completeness. This remains **researcher-elicited self-report**, not independent automated logs or independently metered recommendation adherence; the 2020 83% use report should be attributed to the correct telephone-interview period. The publisher additionally says 6% (two farmers) were dissatisfied, which is not sufficient to reconstruct the 2020 respondent denominator exactly, and the 2019 statement about 46 advice recipients must not be combined arithmetically with the original 35 farms.

**Additional original-text measurement audit.** The openly posted full text (Results, 2017 baseline) states that **74% of participating farmers had no water-consumption records and only 26% used water meters** at that time; this is a baseline equipment/reporting characteristic, **not** a 2020 metering rate. In its 2020 telephone-feedback results, **83% reported using the IDSS, 71% reported regularly checking soil-moisture status, 49% said irrigation duration remained unchanged and 40% reported more frequent but shorter irrigation**. These are different, potentially overlapping self-reported behaviors, not mutually exclusive response categories. Importantly, the **11% who reported not using the IDSS also reported following its reports because they lacked an installed irrigation system**; that should not be flattened into a claim that 11% ignored advice. The paper itself calls for more water meters and for future comparison of advised and non-advised farmers, limiting causal claims about realized water savings. The original-text phrase that **46 farmers used e-mail advice in 2019** remains incompatible with using 35 initially interested farms as a simple person-level denominator; a changing participation roster or unit distinction is plausible but **not established** from the accessible text. This full-text extraction is source-anchored at the [author-posted article](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/343801677_Farmers_Try_to_Improve_Their_Irrigation_Practices_by_Using_Daily_Irrigation_Recommendations-The_Vipava_Valley_Case_Slovenia), Methods and Results pp. 4–11; it does not resolve the missing respondent denominator.

Crucially, the article's **25% projected irrigation-volume reduction and 24% projected energy and CO₂ differences are conditional scenario simulations**, not measured realized savings attributable to actual farmer adoption. An unresolved publisher-text statement referring to "Forty-six farmers" alongside 35 initially interested farms deserves an explicit source-check before any person-based numerator/denominator or adoption calculation is used. Source: https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4395/10/9/1238; detailed extraction `PRIMARY_SOURCE_CVEJIC_2020_FIELD_ADOPTION_PROVENANCE_2026-10-25.md`.

### 3.3 Field inputs and virtual human decisions in Nebraska
Jamal and coauthors (2023, DOI **10.1029/2023WR035810**) combine real field observations for maize irrigation with optimized, modeled schedules and a virtual farmer decision workshop. The two-field comparison illustrates potential scheduling contrasts, while the virtual group exercise elicits choices under study-specific assumptions. Farmer workshop participation, actual field inputs and virtual selection outcomes must be treated as **different evidence units**; a counterfactual optimized schedule cannot be presented as achieved savings from field deployment. The study's documentation differentiates ten workshop attendees from two groups of four in the decision exercise. The group schedules and predicted returns should not be treated as eight independent on-farm trial replications. Source: https://doi.org/10.1029/2023WR035810; extraction `CYCLE10_05_JAMAL_2023_PRIMARY_EXTRACTION.md`.

### 3.4 Experimental-field water savings without demonstrated farmer uptake: Lebanon
Abi Saab, Jomaa, Skaf, Fahed and Todorovic (2019; DOI **10.3390/w11020252**) evaluated the Bluleaf smartphone advisory system in durum wheat during two growing seasons at the **Tal Amara experimental station of the Lebanese Agricultural Research Institute**, not a sample of independently operating commercial farmers. The trial comprised **nine 300-m² plots** in a randomized complete block arrangement: three irrigation treatments (Bluleaf schedule, customary farmer-style schedule, and rainfed) with three plot replicates each. The authors describe treatment-specific valves and flow meters, soil-water monitoring, grain-yield measurements and a comparison of modeled versus observed soil-water depletion. Reported gross irrigation was **270 versus 360 mm** for Bluleaf versus the customary schedule in 2016–2017 and **331 versus 450 mm** in 2017–2018. Those reported seasonal contrasts correspond to **25.0%** and **26.4%** less gross irrigation, respectively, relative to their respective customary comparators; the publisher abstract reports **25.7%** overall and more than 1000 m³/ha saved. The abstract's pooled figure should be retained as the author's reported value rather than recomputed from rounded seasonal numbers without the underlying exact plot-level data.

This study is particularly useful because **physical experimental-field irrigation outcomes and model-performance metrics are available** (M and P), whereas the experimental-station design does **not establish independent farmer acceptance, actual farmer adherence or post-project voluntary use** (O/S not established from the inspected methods). Its randomized plots strengthen a site-level agronomic comparison but cannot be counted as nine independent farms or as evidence of widespread technology adoption. Primary publisher text, sections 2.2.2 and 3.1: https://www.mdpi.com/2073-4441/11/2/252 . **Full open publisher text inspected 2026-10-25; individual table values/uncertainty still require final author verification.**

### 3.5 Advice-versus-action patterns in Australia: SMS logs, grower input and economic constraints
Car, Christen, Hornbuckle and Moore (2012; DOI **10.1016/j.compag.2012.03.003**) tested IrriSat SMS with **72 participating irrigators** over a roughly 200-day growing season. The original institutional abstract reports **45** who sent irrigation/rainfall messages throughout the season, **13** who stopped around halfway, and **14** who never returned messages (45/72 = 62.5%, rounded to 63%). These describe **data-return participation**, not independently metered compliance with each recommendation. The study is more informative than an abstract-only reading suggests: a **full article text deposited by coauthor Evan Christen** was located and inspected, including Methods, Results Sections 3.3–3.5, Tables 4–6 and Figures 5–6 (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257018309_Using_a_mobile_phone_Short_Messaging_Service_SMS_for_irrigation_scheduling_in_Australia_-_Farmers%27_participation_and_utility_evaluation). Its original journal identity is corroborated by the University of New England institutional record (https://rune.une.edu.au/entities/publication/68f9d1ef-6e62-415a-b831-0beb96df9853).

The investigators recorded incoming SMS timestamps, used participants' reported irrigation and rainfall inputs in water-balance calculations, and interviewed irrigators before and after the season. A single irrigation management unit was chosen for each participant, and researchers helped train users and checked inactive accounts. The article's Figures 5–6 and six descriptive user categories distinguish participants irrigating close to, above, or below advice; **68% is reported for a broad engagement category, not for strict recommendation adherence**. The authors explicitly report that **53/72 (74%)** said they actively considered DSS advice, while **79%** described combining it with other information sources. These percentages address overlapping, different survey constructs; they must not be summed or treated as measured conservation. Some farmers deliberately applied less water for crop quality, profitability or soil reasons; deviations from advice are therefore not necessarily evidence of irrationality or DSS failure.

**Source-level caution:** the machine-readable rendering of Table 5 appears to yield category counts inconsistent with its stated total of 72; the figure/table page image must be checked before citing any reconstructed category numerator. The source also discusses return sign-up for a later season, but willingness or enrollment is not independently observed post-support sustained practice. Although grower-input records permit descriptive advice-versus-reported-irrigation comparisons, they do not establish **independently calibrated event-level metering, a randomized causal water-saving effect, or validated long-term adoption**. This case directly challenges the idea that advice–action comparison itself is new: the present manuscript's proposed worksheet must add provenance and inference controls beyond analyses already demonstrated in 2012.

### 3.6 Commercial-farm agronomic outcomes with unresolved long-term uptake: Italy
Elia, Lazzizera, La Rotonda and Conversa (2022; DOI **10.17660/ActaHortic.2022.1335.81**) report a **six-commercial-farm** 2018 trial of GesCoN fertigation advice for processing tomato. According to the publisher's abstract, each farm had a DSS-guided plot and a farmer-managed comparison plot; growers received daily smartphone notifications. The authors report an average **24% reduction in seasonal irrigation water volume** and **7% reduction in nitrogen fertilizer**, without an observed effect on marketable or total yield, and favorable farmer feedback. Unlike the Lebanon research-station study, the setting includes commercial growers and management of advice on farms. Nevertheless, a single growing season of researcher-supported use plus favorable perceptions does **not establish multi-season voluntary adoption**; the accessible abstract alone cannot verify measurement calibration, treatment allocation, uncertainty, or plot-level event compliance.

This example contributes a distinct **field outcome + grower feedback** configuration, while its source limitations prohibit pooling the reported 24% with Lebanon's 25.7% or Cvejić's modeled 25% as though all three were measured and exchangeable. Primary abstract: https://www.actahort.org/books/1335/1335_81.htm . **Publisher abstract checked 2026-10-25; full article not yet obtained.**

### 3.7 Directly documented implementation gap in Germany: comparative user survey
Baroni, Drastig, Lichtenfeld, Jost and Claas (2019; DOI **10.1002/ird.2337**) surveyed users and compared **six irrigation-scheduling methods** in Germany. The Wiley publisher abstract explicitly states that recommendations were **rarely implemented**, even though user feedback was positive and systems gave broadly consistent *seasonal* irrigation requirements; larger differences arose in irrigation timing and event counts. This is a particularly strong counterexample to any claim that perceived usefulness, prediction agreement or provision of advice guarantees action. However, the publicly inspected publisher abstract does **not** quantify the exact number of independent farm-level advice events implemented, field-metered water conservation attributable to adherence, or persistence after support ended. Its findings should therefore be coded **S** (reported feedback) and an implementation-gap observation with **O/M not independently verified at event level from the abstract**, not a new water-savings effect size. It also establishes that the uptake gap had been empirically discussed before this review; the present manuscript must not claim to have discovered it. Original publisher: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ird.2337 . **Full text and supplementary methods not yet independently checked; provisional inclusion as an illustrative contrast, not a verified quantitative synthesis.**

### 3.8 Earlier direct comparison of advice and actual irrigation: a major prior-art constraint
Lorite, García-Vila, Carmona, Santos and Soriano (2012; DOI **10.1007/s11269-012-0023-3**) evaluated a local irrigation advisory service in the Genil–Cabra irrigation scheme in southern Spain during its **first five years**. Unlike studies that measure only satisfaction or model output, this work explicitly assessed acceptance of recommendations using the **Annual Relative Irrigation Supply** relative to advisory recommendations (ARIS_LIAS: actual irrigation applied relative to LIAS advice). The publisher abstract reports crop-dependent ratios of approximately **0.23 for wheat and sunflower** and **0.94 for maize**, with substantial between-farmer heterogeneity, interpreted by the authors as limited uptake of advisory prescriptions. It also considered irrigation water productivity and benefit in monetary units, demonstrating that the economics of recommended schedules can affect acceptance. These are original authors' published estimates, not new observations by this review.

**Interpretation:** This 2012 study is a particularly direct predecessor for comparing *recommendation versus actual irrigation*. Therefore neither the existence of an implementation gap nor quantitative recommendation-to-application comparison can be claimed as an invention of the present review. A possible narrower addition would be an **event-resolved, provenance-tagged audit** linking advice, action, measured water, credible counterfactual and post-project persistence, with explicit uncertainty and heterogeneous outcomes. The inspected Springer page is a subscription-content preview: the precise data-acquisition instruments, individual event denominators, inferential model and follow-up independence require authorized full-text examination. A ratio below one does not by itself prove technical noncompliance: intentional deficit irrigation, supply constraints, profit optimization or altered crop conditions can explain departures. Source: [Springer publisher record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11269-012-0023-3).

## 4. Critical argument: from model validity to implementation validity
These contrasting cases make one methodological problem particularly clear: irrigation DSS research frequently combines more than one observational layer within a single publication. A field trial may contain direct water measurements and subjective farmer ratings. A multi-year initiative may report sustained advisory exposure and simulated scenario potential while lacking independently documented farmer irrigation events. A virtual scheduling exercise may elicit realistic decisions while remaining hypothetical in terms of physical irrigation delivered to the farm.

A useful critical review should therefore map **which layer underwrites each conclusion**, rather than assign an entire paper an undifferentiated "real-world validation" or "adoption" designation. The most demanding water-savings claim would ideally link a documented recommendation, a verified farmer implementation event, field-metered water and agronomic measurements, a transparent comparator and adequate follow-up, with sources of confounding addressed. Failing any such element need not negate the study's narrower contribution: a model can still be useful for system design, and farmer self-reports can still inform usability and implementation barriers. Problems arise when evidence valid for one construct is generalized to another.

### 4.1 A three-way inference mismatch: field outcomes, engagement and scenarios
Across the eight illustrative cases, at least three fundamentally different propositions are being tested. The Lebanese randomized-plot study and the Italian commercial-farm paired comparison address **how water inputs and agronomic outcomes differed under two implemented management strategies**, subject to their design-specific limitations. The Australian SMS trial addresses **whether farmers continued to return operational information when advice was delivered through a low-friction channel**; the primary accessible record does not demonstrate water conservation. The Slovenian study contains **farmer-reported advisory use alongside conditional modeled savings**, which should not be merged into an observed-water-savings estimate. Amini combines local physical comparisons with participatory assessments; Jamal uses measured field inputs alongside virtual human decision-making. These contrasts challenge the assumption that any single reported percentage can represent "DSS success" across studies.

An appropriate comparison matrix therefore needs **separate columns for the measured unit (plot/farm/person/irrigation event), exposure and advice delivery, verified action, water metering or model output, comparator, follow-up horizon, and the source document's evidentiary completeness**. Where original full text is inaccessible, the classification is provisional and the denominator or precision is withheld. Even for a randomized research plot, the independent experimental unit is the plot or block, **not a farmer**. Conversely, frequent farmer data submission may indicate engagement without documenting advice-specific adherence.

### 4.2 A falsifiable reporting standard rather than a retrospective score
The proposed six-link claim-to-evidence chain should be framed as a **prospective reporting template**, not a validated quantitative instrument or universally original taxonomy. Each link has a potential observation and a failure mode: recommendation timestamp and agronomic context; verifiable delivery; actual farmer action; field-measured outcome; credible counterfactual with uncertainty; and repeated voluntary use after support ends. Missing evidence narrows what can be inferred, but is **not evidence of treatment failure**. A later methodological validation could assess inter-rater reproducibility of source classification, prediction of independent field outcomes, and incremental explanatory value compared with existing evaluation/adoption frameworks. No such validation has yet been conducted.

A checkmark in a paper-wide evidence category must never be used to infer that *every* reported endpoint is measured. **Interpretation:** For a causal or quasi-causal claim about realized conservation, the unit of analysis should be an identifiable advice event (time, field and recommended volume), its independently observed implementation, a measured irrigation outcome, a valid comparator and follow-up. This criterion is proposed as a *reporting standard*, not an instrument already validated across the literature. Repeated software logins, self-reported usefulness and experimentally enforced irrigation each answer different questions. A further complication from Ara et al. (2021) is that farmers may internalize a rule and stop using the DSS: genuine **learned-heuristic adoption** requires a distinct observational definition and must not be inferred solely from discontinued software use.

### 4.4 A deliberately adversarial novelty test: Lorite (2012), Ara (2021) and Baroni (2019)
The review's conceptual scope is constrained by three distinct predecessors. Lorite et al. directly quantified differences between advice and actual applied irrigation across crops over five years; Ara et al. discussed the adoption of decision heuristics even when software use stops; Baroni et al. documented favorable attitudes alongside infrequent implementation. Together these works invalidate any sweeping claim that prior scholarship ignored farmer behavior, recommendation compliance, or the distinction between system use and agronomic practice. The proposed contribution is instead **traceable epistemic discipline**: for each published conclusion, the reviewer should identify the exact observation supporting it, avoid swapping participant and plot denominators, and label simulated and self-reported outcomes before making causal or adoption claims. Whether this narrower contribution is publishable remains an editorial and author-led scientific judgment, not a demonstrated new metric.

The strongest falsification test for the proposed reporting worksheet is a published paper that already documents recommendation timestamps, farmer irrigation events, metered volumes, counterfactual design, uncertainty and independent post-support follow-up. Such a source would reduce or eliminate the worksheet's claimed incremental value. A future validation study should compare independent human coders' reproducibility and whether the worksheet changes editorial decisions; neither test has been performed here. It would be incorrect to assign this AI-assisted classification the status of a validated measurement scale.

### 4.5 Claim-level audit with admissible and inadmissible conclusions

A reproducible critical review must be able to reject a conclusion even when the original paper is relevant and its narrower finding is credible. Table 2 therefore audits **specific statements rather than rating entire studies**. The table is an interpretive worked example, not a new experiment, a validated adherence instrument, a meta-analysis or an assertion that the underlying authors made the inadmissible claim. For rows supported only by an abstract, the source-access restriction is itself part of the result.

**What this exercise does and does not establish.** The eight rows expose three different errors that would otherwise survive an undifferentiated narrative: (1) replacing observed water application with a simulated scenario; (2) replacing farmer-reported engagement with independently verified implementation; and (3) replacing a farm/plot/event denominator with a different person- or platform-level denominator. The exercise also shows that the available evidence is **insufficient to estimate how common those errors are in the wider literature**, because cases were purposively chosen and some sources are abstract-only. It cannot validate M/O/S/P/U as a reliable coding instrument; any inter-coder agreement or external predictive validity would require an actual independent study.

The strongest prior-art challenge is more extensive than a single paper: **Baroni et al. (2019) explicitly cite Lorite et al. (2012) in the Wiley-hosted reference list**. Thus, the earlier actual-versus-recommended irrigation comparison was already integrated into later implementation-gap scholarship. Ara (2021), Bounajra (2024), Wei (2024) and Raffa (2025) further narrow any broad adoption or evaluation novelty. The defensible *candidate* contribution is therefore only the auditable **claim-level synthesis demonstrated in Table 2**, not discovery of the implementation gap or invention of adherence measurement.

None of these abstract checks should be misrepresented as full-text review.

### 4.6 Selection-bias sensitivity and the minimum defensible novelty claim

The purposive cases span **four independent contrasts**: experimental versus commercial farm context; measured versus modeled irrigation outcomes; direct behavioral logs versus farmer self-report; and a single season versus a multiyear advisory service. Selection on these axes makes methodological contrasts interpretable but does **not** guarantee a representative sample. Studies with successful adoption, null outcomes, abandoned services or strong independent follow-up may be underrepresented. In particular, if a previously published review already uses event-level source traceability and a counterfactual-aware claim audit, this manuscript's proposed reporting worksheet offers little conceptual novelty; a journal editor could reasonably reject it on that basis. The author must therefore explicitly choose whether the contribution is a focused **Perspective on evidence-reporting errors**, rather than imply a comprehensive systematic review or a novel validated scientific instrument.

## 5. Research implications and a minimum reporting worksheet
Future reporting should specify the unit and denominator of each claim: farmer, farm, field, treatment plot, irrigation event or simulated season. Authors should distinguish assignment from actual adherence, document the source and calibration of measured water delivery, preserve negative and heterogeneous outcomes, and explain the environmental or economic conditions assumed in counterfactual analyses. For adoption, actual duration of use and participation after the research team's active support ends deserve explicit measurement. At the publication level, linked data and calculation materials would make discrepancies easier to resolve and improve reader trust.


### 5.1 Minimum claim-to-evidence reporting worksheet (proposal; not validated)
For every quantitative or adoption statement, record **(i)** verbatim claim and exact source table/page; **(ii)** study unit (farmer, plot, field, advice event or simulated season), sampling denominator and missingness; **(iii)** whether the recommendation was generated and delivered with a timestamp; **(iv)** the basis for implementation status (independent observation, automated meter/log, researcher interview, farmer diary, or unknown); **(v)** water outcome instrument, units, calibration, measurement period and whether volume is measured or modeled; **(vi)** comparison group or counterfactual assumptions, uncertainty, confounding and any adverse or null sites; and **(vii)** evidence of continued voluntary behavior after study assistance. A source can receive multiple M/O/S/P/U labels for distinct claims; the labels must not be aggregated into an unvalidated score.

The Amini F5 and F7 contrast illustrates why adverse cases must be preserved: published irrigation depths indicate **363.2 versus 616.8 mm** (treatment/control) at F5 but **1057.6 versus 764.9 mm** at F7. Thus, the treatment-minus-control contrasts are **−253.6 mm** and **+292.7 mm**, respectively. These are within-source arithmetic differences from rounded values, not estimates of causal population effects. The Slovenian 25% scenario and the Australian 45/72 SMS data-return statistic address different constructs and cannot be pooled with these field-depth differences. The Spanish ARIS_LIAS comparison demonstrates that recommendation–action ratios predate this manuscript; the worksheet's possible value lies in its **explicit source-to-claim audit trail**, not in the ratio itself.

### 5.2 Consequences for trial design and reporting
Researchers can strengthen field inference by prespecifying the independent unit of allocation and analysis, recording recommendation exposure separately from observed irrigation events, calibrating meters, and retaining both negative and positive outcomes. A sustained-adoption endpoint requires a time horizon beyond active researcher assistance and an operational definition that can accommodate learned heuristics without mistaking discontinued logins for failure. Where controlled comparisons are infeasible, uncertainty and plausible confounding should be stated rather than converted into a spurious causal effect. Financial and water-access constraints should be recorded because departures from recommendations may be rational management choices.

## 6. Limitations and validity boundaries
The illustrative evidence sources were chosen to illustrate contrasting research designs, not to estimate the prevalence of successful irrigation-DSS deployments across global agriculture. The bibliographic pilot is not an exhaustive interdisciplinary search, a human-screened review, or a final eligible study corpus. The Elia (2022), Baroni (2019) and Lorite (2012) source analyses are constrained by abstract/preview access; Car (2012) was newly inspected in a coauthor-deposited full-text copy, with a table-transcription anomaly still awaiting visual resolution. Original full-text audits for Mabhaudhi (2023) and Umutoni (2024) also remain incomplete. Numerical and denominator inconsistencies remain open; the original rounded Amini values cannot be used to reconstruct unreported raw observations. The 2012 Spanish study is a substantive prior-art challenge, not an incidental citation. This is not an empirical validation of the proposed worksheet, a meta-analysis, or a systematic prevalence estimate. The present text is AI-assisted and requires substantive independent scientific inspection, interpretation and revision by the intended human author. No independent second human reviewer has been claimed. The named human author must materially select, inspect, interpret and revise the scientific evidence and assume accountability; AI drafting is not human authorship.
