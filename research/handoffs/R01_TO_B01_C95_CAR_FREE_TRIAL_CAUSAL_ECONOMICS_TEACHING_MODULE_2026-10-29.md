## Ready-to-integrate English teaching passage (original editorial writing)
**Why free trials do not identify a commercial adoption curve.** A producer may continue receiving an irrigation decision-support message when the service is provided at no charge, even if the same producer would decline a paid subscription. A free research trial can reveal whether the interface is usable and whether participants report consulting its recommendations. It cannot, by itself, identify a demand schedule for a paid service, an economically optimal price, or the causal effect of advice on harvested output. A credible commercial evaluation must distinguish the communication cost borne by participants from the full service fee, quantify training and labor costs, and measure actual choices when prices vary under a transparent design. When growers recount a weather-related crop loss after a heatwave, their account is valuable qualitative evidence about perceived usefulness and risk, but it is not a substitute for a prespecified comparator and independent agronomic measurements.

## Original synthetic worked exercise — *not* Car field data
A hypothetical farm considers a decision-support subscription. Assume for classroom arithmetic only: annual subscription **S=$150**, 87 messages at **$0.25** (so **M=$21.75**), two hours of user time at **$15/h** (so **L=$30**), and an uncertain total *incremental* farm benefit **B** ranging from **$0 to $250**. Define incremental net benefit **NB = B − S − M − L**, ignoring discounting for a single season. Then the fixed incremental cost is **$201.75**, the benefit break-even is **B > $201.75**, and **NB ranges from −$201.75 to +$48.25** over the stipulated benefit interval. With a hypothetical **zero** subscription fee and all else unchanged, break-even benefit is **B > $51.75**. This comparison does **not** estimate real Car participant behavior, price elasticity, water saving or profit. The scenario deliberately shows why a positive opinion in a free trial cannot certify a positive paid-service net benefit.

**Student questions:** (1) Which of S, M, L and B were actually measured in the original Car trial? (2) Why is the observed absence of SMS-cost complaints under free service not an estimate of subscription demand? (3) What independent field outcome and control design would be needed to infer the heatwave effect? (4) Why is a 2-hour labor valuation in this exercise not the same as the original authors' assumed ~116 minutes?

**Answer key:** (1) Only the paper's illustrative message count/tariff and its modeled effort are source-reported; S, B and the $15/h wage are invented for this exercise. (2) There is no observed positive subscription price variation or revealed preference under paid terms. (3) Predefined comparator, baseline comparability or defensible assignment, calibrated water and crop/yield outcomes, weather exposure and uncertainty. (4) 2h is a rounded teaching assumption, not an independent validation of the authors' time model.
