## Directly usable teaching insert (editor may adapt with attribution)

**What a low-cost digital advisory trial does—and does not—prove.** Car et al. (2012) studied an Australian irrigation-advice service delivered by SMS. In the published trial, participation itself was free; growers paid for outgoing messages. The article gives a high-message example of 87 SMS messages at AUD 0.25 each, or AUD 21.75 in that trial's currency and period. Interviews did not identify message charges as an obstacle among participating growers. This supports a narrow statement about *participant SMS transaction expense in the observed trial*, not a claim that the complete irrigation-advice service cost AUD 21.75 per farm, nor that it generated positive net profit. The article explicitly points to a separate reference for commercial system costs. A defensible enterprise budget must additionally specify service/subscription cost, installation and calibration, connectivity, staff time, maintenance, crop-specific benefits, counterfactual irrigation volume, yield quality, prices and uncertainty. The source also describes scheduling comparisons in *dripper run-time minutes*, not independently measured water-volume savings; therefore neither a return-on-investment percentage nor a physical water-saving effect can be calculated from this trial's SMS charge alone.

## Editorial exercise with no fabricated empirical data

For a *hypothetical* farm, define annual net benefit as **Δrevenue + verified water/energy savings − subscription − SMS − installation amortization − maintenance − incremental labour**. Require students to state prices, units, baseline and counterfactual and run low/base/high sensitivity scenarios. The AUD 21.75 source example may be used **only as a historical illustration**, not as an estimated annual cost for the hypothetical farm. No numerical profitability conclusion is supplied.
