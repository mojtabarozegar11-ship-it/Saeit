# A transparent research-collaboration brief: evidence, methods and deliverables

Researchers and commercial partners often need a short, reliable way to assess a proposed collaboration. A polished résumé is useful, but it cannot replace a clear problem statement, an inspectable method and a record of what has actually been completed.

This open brief outlines a practical framework for discussing agricultural economics, research methods, data-informed enterprise systems and digital product development.

## 1. Define the decision before proposing a solution

A useful project begins with a decision that someone must make. Examples include selecting a production scenario under water constraints, evaluating the economics of a processing line, or determining whether a digital workflow reduces manual errors. State who will use the outcome and which alternatives they currently consider.

Distinguish a *research question* from a *product requirement*. “Does irrigation scheduling improve outcomes under defined conditions?” is a research question. “Can a farm manager compare three irrigation scenarios with transparent inputs?” is a product requirement. They need different evidence and acceptance criteria.

## 2. Separate five levels of evidence

1. **Idea:** a proposed topic without verified implementation.
2. **Design:** a documented protocol, model or prototype specification.
3. **Execution:** recorded data collection or a functioning system.
4. **Validation:** independent or reproducible evaluation against stated criteria.
5. **Publication or adoption:** a traceable journal record, public release or documented real-world use.

These stages must not be treated as interchangeable. A project page, a repository or a draft manuscript does not demonstrate peer-reviewed publication. A successful automated test does not establish customer adoption.

## 3. Specify data and quality controls

For a research project, define the population, sampling method, variables, exclusion rules, missing-data handling, analytical approach and uncertainty measures. For software, define supported inputs, access controls, reproducible test cases, error states, maintenance responsibilities and rollback procedures.

Before discussing large-scale deployment, identify the smallest credible evidence package: a representative sample, a bounded implementation and a documented comparison against the existing workflow.

## 4. Create an honest collaboration offer

A short proposal can contain:

- The business or scientific problem and intended beneficiaries.
- A bounded deliverable with an acceptance test.
- Required data, equipment, permissions and expertise.
- Ownership of background intellectual property and new outputs.
- Confidentiality, privacy and publication-review arrangements.
- A milestone at which both parties can decide whether to continue.

Avoid claiming institutional partnerships, facilities, credentials or guaranteed outcomes unless they are independently supported.

## 6. A practical first meeting agenda

A 30-minute exploratory meeting can focus on the problem and intended decision (10 minutes), available evidence and constraints (10 minutes), and a draft acceptance test plus responsibilities (10 minutes). Close by recording open questions rather than implying that a contract, funding or institutional endorsement already exists.

A credible collaboration grows from clear evidence, honest uncertainty and a small deliverable that can be checked independently.
