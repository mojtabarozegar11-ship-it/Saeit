# An 84-day chess improvement plan: how to make practice measurable

A chess improvement plan works when it turns general ambitions into observable decisions. Studying openings or solving puzzles can be useful, but progress is difficult to evaluate without a baseline, a repeatable review method and a record of recurring mistakes.

This independent study companion introduces a practical approach for adult learners, club players and coaches. It is educational guidance, not a promise of a rating increase.

## 1. Record a baseline before choosing exercises

Select ten recent games played at your normal time control. For each, record the phase in which the decisive error occurred, the time available, and whether the mistake involved calculation, candidate moves, king safety, endgame technique or time management. Do not label every loss an opening problem. A poor result may originate much later.

Write one sentence about the critical position *before* using an engine: “I considered moves A and B but overlooked the opponent’s forcing reply.” Only then compare your reasoning with analysis. This separates a calculation failure from a knowledge gap.

## 2. Organize 84 days into four evidence-based blocks

**Days 1–21 — Observation and calculation.** Solve a small set of positions under a consistent time limit. Record candidate moves, the opponent's strongest replies and your final choice. Accuracy alone is insufficient: also record why an incorrect move looked attractive.

**Days 22–42 — Position evaluation and planning.** Use positions from your own games to practice identifying king safety, pawn structure, piece activity and immediate tactical threats. Explain one candidate plan and the condition under which you would change it.

**Days 43–63 — Practical play and endings.** Play serious training games, then review critical transitions into simplified positions. Focus on endgame principles that actually arise in your games, not a large catalogue of rare studies.

**Days 64–84 — Integration and review.** Repeat a comparable set of exercises and review the same categories as the baseline. Compare the quality of your decisions, not only your win percentage.

## 3. Use a human-first game review

Immediately after a game, mark three positions where you felt uncertain. For each, answer:

1. What did I think my opponent wanted?
2. Which forcing moves did I calculate?
3. Which candidate move did I reject, and why?
4. What information would have changed my decision?
5. What will I practice before the next game?

An engine can reveal tactical mistakes, but it cannot directly reconstruct what you noticed at the board. Preserve your original reasoning before checking engine evaluations.

## 4. Measure improvement without inventing certainty

Choose a few repeatable indicators: percentage of training positions solved correctly, proportion of critical positions with at least two documented candidate moves, average time spent on difficult decisions and number of repeated error types across ten games.

Use similar difficulty and time controls when comparing periods. A short improvement in puzzle scores does not by itself establish an increase in tournament playing strength. Competition results depend on opponents, sample size and many other variables.

## 5. Make the schedule sustainable

A short daily session is often easier to sustain than an ambitious schedule that collapses after one week. For example, allocate time to a small calculation set, one annotated game fragment and a brief error log. On busy days, preserve the review habit rather than rushing through many positions without feedback.

If a concept remains unclear, reduce the complexity of the exercise and work with a coach or reliable reference before advancing.

## 7. A one-page progress record

For each week, keep a record of the date, exercise category, position source, your initial choice, the strongest opposing reply, the review conclusion and the next targeted practice task. At the end of each 21-day block, summarize your three most common error patterns and select only one or two for focused correction.

A training plan is successful when it helps you recognize errors sooner and make more consistent decisions—not when it promises effortless mastery.
