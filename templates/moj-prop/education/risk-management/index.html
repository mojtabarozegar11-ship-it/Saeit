Risk management is a process, not a prediction

How to apply a coherent risk budget to a simulated trading evaluation, without mistaking a virtual balance for money or a practice result for a guaranteed return.

Simulation and reward disclosure: This library provides education, not investment advice or a guarantee of earnings. The advertised $1,000 balance is virtual; no customer trading capital is accepted. Any real performance reward is conditional on verified qualifying profit after a successful evaluation and complete accepted terms. Payment and payout functionality must be verified independently before sending funds.

1. Start with the amount you can lose, not the amount you hope to earn

A simulated evaluation measures decisions against a published rule set. The displayed account value is virtual: it is neither a cash deposit nor money a participant may withdraw. Before selecting a trade, record the virtual reference balance, the permitted loss threshold, an invalidation point and all expected transaction costs. A profitable outcome does not cure a breach of a risk rule.

2. Convert percentage limits into a planned risk budget

For the advertised MOJ PROP Starter 30 example, a $1,000 virtual starting balance makes a 3% per-trade ceiling equal to $30, a 4% aggregate open-position ceiling equal to $40, and a 6% daily drawdown percentage equal to $60 when applied to that same reference amount. These are illustrations, not an authoritative definition of daily equity resets, stop calculations or all final pass criteria.

Illustrative per-trade risk budget = reference equity × risk percentage

Each rule applies independently. Two open positions that each risk $30 would imply $60 in aggregate planned risk and would exceed the $40 aggregate ceiling, even though each trade individually meets the $30 limit.

3. Measure combined exposure, not just individual orders

List every open simulated position and its planned loss at invalidation. Add those amounts before considering another entry. Positions in correlated instruments can lose simultaneously; correlation does not eliminate the contractual aggregate limit. If existing open exposure totals $26 in the $1,000 illustration, no more than $14 of additional planned loss remains under the 4% aggregate ceiling, before any other constraint.

4. Treat drawdown as a separate account-level control

Per-order stops and daily account drawdown answer different questions. An unrealized loss, a realized loss, trading costs, and the time at which a day resets can affect the account-level measure. The final published terms must define whether equity, balance, or a specified reference value governs. Do not assume a simple percentage example determines how the actual engine scores breaches.

5. Protect the process against exceptional conditions

In live markets, slippage, spreads, partial fills and temporary lack of liquidity can create losses beyond an anticipated stop level. A simulator may approximate these effects differently. Record the cost assumptions, and avoid treating simulated execution quality as a forecast of achievable brokerage results.

6. Use a written pre-trade and post-trade checklist

  • Instrument, trade thesis, planned entry, stop and exit criteria.
  • Maximum planned loss in virtual dollars and as a percentage.
  • Aggregate exposure after the new order, including existing positions.
  • Remaining daily drawdown capacity, based on the applicable rule definitions.
  • Estimated fees, spread, timing and event risks.
  • After exit: realized simulation result, rule compliance and a concise reflection.

Skipping a marginal trade is a legitimate risk-management decision. Never enlarge positions to recover earlier simulated losses or to force an evaluation pass.

7. What should a participant verify before purchasing?

Read the exact evaluation terms covering overall drawdown, daily reset, profit targets, minimum trading days, minimum trade count, disallowed activity and dispute handling. Confirm that a genuine, server-issued payment invoice is available and that the reward method and deadline are written and verifiable. An educational page or an accessible checkout form is not proof that a transfer or payout has occurred.
